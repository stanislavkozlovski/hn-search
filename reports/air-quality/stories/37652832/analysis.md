# [Air Quality Monitors from USD \$169 to \$6k use the same low-cost PM Modules](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37652832)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants discuss comparisons showing that air-quality monitors at very different prices may use the same low-cost particulate modules. They debate what sensor components can tell buyers about overall product value, the need for independent testing, and how calibration and environmental conditions affect readings. AirGradient’s open-source claims and product promotion draw criticism and responses about licensing, firmware quality, community support, and the value of its hardware. Other threads compare monitors, discuss CO₂ and VOC sensing, radon, filtration, and using readings to control ventilation or HVAC.
Tags: air quality monitors, particulate sensors, sensor modules, product testing, AirGradient, open source, CO₂, HVAC control

Comments compare particulate monitor modules and question calibration, independent validation, and sensor lifespan. The thread also discusses CO2's possible cognitive effects and ventilation role, cooking-related PM spikes, and filtration and ventilation tradeoffs. ## Arguments

- clairity [[./thread#^c37661366|clairity]] argues that particulate pollution deserves household monitoring because of respiratory and cardiovascular harm, citing an estimated million-plus premature deaths annually from pollution. These are general health claims in the comment, not findings established by the supplied material or measurements of participants’ exposure.
- vGPU [[./thread#^c37684085|vGPU]] argues for concern about household CO2, claiming that most studies find cognitive declines beginning around 800–1,200 ppm. They also cite others’ reports of nighttime levels exceeding 2,000 ppm and studies of multifamily housing exceeding 1,000 ppm. These are secondhand claims, not their own readings.
- amluto [[./thread#^c37662440|amluto]] argues that CO2 remains useful even if its direct cognitive effects are disputed: it can indicate the proportion of exhaled air indoors and guide demand-controlled ventilation. This separates the practical value of measuring ventilation from proving that CO2 itself causes impairment.
- Retric [[./thread#^c37660618|Retric]] argues that monitoring verifies whether filtration actually works. For wildfire smoke, Retric [[./thread#^c37661382|Retric]] suggests existing filtration may be insufficient and extra filters attached to fans may be necessary. This is a proposed response to exposure, without a firsthand wildfire experiment or quantified improvement.
- bearjaws [[./thread#^c37660519|bearjaws]] argues that someone considering a monitor costing over \$400 should consider spending on purification instead, claiming HEPA plus carbon filtration will address '99\%' of the problem unless a specific molecule is of concern. The percentage is an unsupported estimate, not a measured removal result.
- stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] proposes filtered incoming air and positive pressure where polluted outdoor air continually infiltrates a building. Their argument is that controlling incoming pollution can be more practical than requiring recirculating purifiers to overcome unrestricted infiltration.

## Counterarguments

- clairity [[./thread#^c37661366|clairity]] categorically rejects concern about CO2 in homes and offices, reserving concern for much higher industrial concentrations and viewing IKEA’s omission of CO2 monitoring positively. This directly conflicts with the household cognitive-risk claims made by vGPU [[./thread#^c37684085|vGPU]]; neither position is resolved by study results supplied here.
- \_2d30 [[./thread#^c37660294|\_2d30]] challenges the implication that switching from gas to induction will eliminate cooking PM2.5, arguing that food produces particles regardless of heat source. LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37663816|LeifCarrotson]] responds by considering testing and improving the exhaust fan. There is no reported induction-versus-gas comparison.
- Retric [[./thread#^c37660618|Retric]] challenges the broad purification argument because humidity and carbon monoxide still warrant monitoring and are not addressed by HEPA plus carbon filtration. These concerns involve different hazards from particulate exposure; the comment does not establish that every home needs an expensive multipollutant monitor.
- aidenn0 [[./thread#^c37661033|aidenn0]] counters the claim that smoke cannot be filtered, describing particulate and VOC components as targets for HEPA and carbon respectively. The subsequent exchange partly concerns different objectives: aidenn0 [[./thread#^c37661176|aidenn0]] distinguishes exposure management from fire alarms, while Retric [[./thread#^c37661438|Retric]] emphasizes the active fire itself and Retric [[./thread#^c37661853|Retric]] favors whole-house alarm coverage and redundancy.
- ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37661083|ahaucnx]] says carbon filters typically do not last long and recommends monitoring TVOCs. reaperman [[./thread#^c37661226|reaperman]] counters that particular linked carbon filters last over a year at high flow rates. Filter specifications, contaminant loads, and breakthrough measurements are absent, so these claims cannot establish a universal replacement interval.
- jiveturkey [[./thread#^c37660818|jiveturkey]] argues that the PM module is only part of a complete monitor, challenging an inference of equivalent products from shared components. iancmceachern [[./thread#^c37660254|iancmceachern]] observes that many electronics markets use only a few underlying chipsets; common components alone therefore do not settle the value or accuracy of complete monitors.
- FloatArtifact [[./thread#^c37661085|FloatArtifact]] says Awair components cannot be replaced and flags finite sensor lifetimes. merrywhether [[./thread#^c37661844|merrywhether]] provides a counterexample involving an earlier model whose sensor module they replaced successfully. That experience challenges a blanket nonreplaceability claim without establishing repairability of the current Element.

## Assumptions

- The comparison’s scope matters: AirGradient’s ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37660021|ahaucnx]] says monitors were selected from AQ-Spec evaluations because those identify their PM modules. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37660245|ahaucnx]] explains that IKEA was absent from that set, and ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37659452|ahaucnx]] describes further module benchmarking as planned. The discussion does not supply a comprehensive market comparison or the proposed benchmark results.
- Interpreting a CO2 display assumes that the instrument actually measures CO2. amluto [[./thread#^c37662440|amluto]] says cheap instruments commonly estimate CO2 from MOS TVOC sensing, challenging the reverse relationship described by clairity [[./thread#^c37661366|clairity]]. pxx [[./thread#^c37659773|pxx]] likewise distinguishes genuine CO2 sensing from VOC-derived 'equivalent' CO2. The discussion does not resolve the sensing method of every named device.
- Branding and appearance are insufficient validation. nikisweeting [[./thread#^c37659049|nikisweeting]] favors an industrial supplier’s Bosean monitor, while lysp [[./thread#^c37659229|lysp]] suspects generic rebadging from its appearance and documentation. dahart [[./thread#^c37659495|dahart]] replies that the compared listings have different specifications and packaging. None supplies a reference-instrument comparison establishing accuracy or product identity.
- Interpreting the mask experiment as a filtration cutoff assumes that the meters could reliably detect smaller particles and that the apparatus controlled leakage and airflow. RecycledEle [[./thread#^c37665706|RecycledEle]] describes taped tubes, approximate sealing, fans, and PM meters, but gives no sensor specifications or calibration. Their conclusion cannot establish a general lower particle-size limit for filters.
- The supplied measurements are largely qualitative. LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37659510|LeifCarrotson]] reports household PM and CO2 spikes without concentrations, baselines, or durations; rapjr9 [[./thread#^c37668904|rapjr9]] describes particle-count logging without presenting a dataset. The numerical household CO2 claims from vGPU [[./thread#^c37684085|vGPU]] concern studies and other people’s reports. These forms of evidence should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Conditions

- Opening windows has a conditional tradeoff. vGPU [[./thread#^c37684085|vGPU]] suggests window opening can address high CO2 in poorly ventilated homes; stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] emphasizes polluted outdoor air and building leakage; FabHK [[./thread#^c37660969|FabHK]] describes the difficulty of keeping both PM2.5 and CO2 low. Together they support the possibility that ventilation lowers CO2 while worsening particulate exposure when outdoor air is dirty, but provide no paired window-open/window-closed measurements.
- Building pressure and conditioning costs affect proposed improvements. stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] says kitchen and bathroom exhaust can draw polluted air through leaks, potentially requiring substantial purifier flow. Filtered positive-pressure ventilation adds an air-conditioning load; heat- or energy-recovery ventilation is suggested as a way to manage that tradeoff, without an installed-system result.
- Prices refer to different levels of assembly and functionality, and were quoted in September 2023. remlov [[./thread#^c37659273|remlov]] cites a \$138 kit, while LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37659510|LeifCarrotson]] cites approximately \$40 for a bare sensor from a distributor or \$12 overseas. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37660245|ahaucnx]] says CO2 sensing is the most expensive part of their bill of materials, making a PM monitor without CO2 an incomplete price comparison.
- Purchase price and delivery can outweigh headline differences. FloatArtifact [[./thread#^c37661085|FloatArtifact]] describes Awair as costing over \$200, while mr\_november [[./thread#^c37662637|mr\_november]] reports buying during a 25\% discount at about \$155, with free shipping making its delivered price close to AirGradient’s. These are individual historical purchasing conditions, not current prices or evidence of superior sensing.
- DIY affordability depends on assembly skills and tools. LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37659510|LeifCarrotson]] distinguishes a largely assembled kit from older soldering kits. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37659619|ahaucnx]] says the soldering version was temporarily withdrawn because some buyers could not assemble its connectors; LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37663810|LeifCarrotson]], an experienced electrical engineer with suitable equipment, found assembly manageable.
- Automation availability is geographically and financially specific. highclass [[./thread#^c37676558|highclass]] says integrated sensor/HVAC or kitchen-fan systems are installed in high-end new apartments in Korea. quickthrowman [[./thread#^c37661979|quickthrowman]] describes commercial threshold-controlled ventilation and asks about residential furnace-fan relays. Neither supplies personal operating results showing that the proposed household setup reduces both PM2.5 and CO2.

## Lived experiences

- LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37659510|LeifCarrotson]] reports building an AirGradient DIY Pro and seeing PM and CO2 spike throughout the house during natural-gas cooking, prompting consideration of induction. LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37663816|LeifCarrotson]] adds that the exhaust fan is so loud the household usually avoids it. This connects observed pollution events to a concrete comfort barrier, but no stove replacement or exhaust improvement had yet been tested.
- stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] cannot leave the front and most side windows open overnight because morning road noise becomes intrusive. They are interested in heat- or energy-recovery ventilation to obtain more fresh air. This is firsthand evidence of noise limiting ventilation, not a measurement of traffic-related pollution or a demonstrated sleep benefit.
- rapjr9 [[./thread#^c37668904|rapjr9]] describes owning a Dylos laser particle counter with serial output, using Windows graphing software to log particle counts, and periodically clearing dust from its intake. Their additional assessment that Sharp LED sensors are less accurate but useful for dust and smoke is an opinion without comparative results in the excerpt.
- RecycledEle [[./thread#^c37665706|RecycledEle]] reports a pandemic-era experiment placing PM meters in two clear tubes, taping masks between them, and using computer fans to move air. They concluded that their best available filters worked only down to 2.5 microns. The apparatus and conclusion are firsthand, but the experiment’s limitations prevent treating that conclusion as established filtration performance.
- nikisweeting [[./thread#^c37659049|nikisweeting]] says the Bosean monitor seems more accurate than inexpensive Amazon devices they tried, without describing a reference standard. Separately, pxx [[./thread#^c37659773|pxx]] reports poor experience with VOC-derived equivalent CO2. Both are firsthand product impressions, not quantified accuracy assessments.
- M3t0r [[./thread#^c37661303|M3t0r]] reports dissatisfaction with AirGradient firmware, running their own Arduino firmware for about two years, then switching successfully to ESPHome. scottlamb [[./thread#^c37660804|scottlamb]] and margalabargala [[./thread#^c37661171|margalabargala]] also favor AirGradient hardware with ESPHome; snapplebobapple [[./thread#^c37667553|snapplebobapple]] says they flashed it before even trying the original firmware. These accounts demonstrate practical software flexibility, not calibrated measurement accuracy.
- merrywhether [[./thread#^c37661844|merrywhether]] reports replacing an earlier Awair model’s all-in-one sensor module with the matching AliExpress part, using fewer than ten screws and finding the repair easy; the device then booted successfully. This establishes one workable repair, without demonstrating post-repair calibration or applicability to newer models.
- ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37661183|ahaucnx]], speaking from AirGradient’s work with modules, reports substantially more failures with Plantower A003 than with 5003. In the parent exchange, ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37660748|ahaucnx]] tentatively identifies the Davis Airlink module as PMSA003. This is vendor experience without sample sizes, failure rates, or operating conditions.
- an\_account [[./thread#^c37659886|an\_account]] reports researching monitors, building one, and ultimately buying and recommending Awair Element. Their reasons are not supplied; newfonewhodis [[./thread#^c37660696|newfonewhodis]] asks what led to that choice. The account establishes a purchasing preference, not comparative performance.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The central CO2 disagreement remains whether typical household concentrations directly impair cognition, merely indicate other ventilation-related exposures, or merit little concern. clairity [[./thread#^c37661366|clairity]], amluto [[./thread#^c37662440|amluto]], and vGPU [[./thread#^c37684085|vGPU]] occupy different positions. No study methods or firsthand cognitive, clinical, or sleep outcomes are provided; reports of nighttime CO2 accumulation do not themselves demonstrate impaired sleep.
- Independent accuracy remains unsettled. jiveturkey [[./thread#^c37681226|jiveturkey]] requests independent evaluation of AirGradient and questions its emphasis on PM2.5 despite additional particle-size outputs. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37659452|ahaucnx]] promises future module comparisons, and louwrentius [[./thread#^c37661151|louwrentius]] links an IKEA–Dylos comparison without reproducing its findings. The supplied text cannot establish equivalent accuracy across differently priced monitors.
- The Bosean device’s CO2 capability remains ambiguous: pxx [[./thread#^c37659773|pxx]] doubts genuine sensing at its price, bananapub [[./thread#^c37660208|bananapub]] says the device does not claim CO2 sensing, and mikestew [[./thread#^c37660318|mikestew]] points to a screen showing CO2. A displayed value alone does not resolve which sensing technology generates it.
- Intervention effectiveness is not quantified here. LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37663816|LeifCarrotson]] proposes testing the exhaust fan, stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] considers improved ventilation, and Retric [[./thread#^c37661382|Retric]] suggests extra filtration for wildfire smoke. None reports the resulting exposure reduction, operating cost, or health improvement, leaving the best combination for a particular building uncertain.
- NO2 and traffic exposure receive little direct evidence. quickthrowman [[./thread#^c37661979|quickthrowman]] mentions NO2 in commercial controls, while LeifCarrotson [[./thread#^c37659510|LeifCarrotson]] had not yet added the proposed TVOC/NOx module. stephen\_g [[./thread#^c37660571|stephen\_g]] discusses road noise rather than measured traffic pollution. There are no firsthand NO2 concentrations here. jpambrun [[./thread#^c37661953|jpambrun]] additionally requests radon coverage but supplies no exposure readings.
- Monitor support and longevity remain relevant uncertainties. emmanuel\_1234 [[./thread#^c37659703|emmanuel\_1234]] criticizes AirGradient’s source availability and support; ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37659983|ahaucnx]] acknowledges firmware shortcomings and says licensing changed to CC-BY-SA, consistent with the current-file report from CodesInChaos [[./thread#^c37660882|CodesInChaos]]. This answers the specific current-license objection more than the maintenance concern. Separately, ahaucnx [[./thread#^c37670974|ahaucnx]] alleges that Awair disabled first-generation monitors; that competitor’s claim is not independently established in the supplied text.
