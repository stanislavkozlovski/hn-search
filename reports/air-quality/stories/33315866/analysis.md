# [Our buildings are making us sick](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=33315866)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Discussion explores how building design and ventilation affect indoor health, including airtight construction, HVAC, window access, gas appliances, CO2, radon, and filtration. Participants debate green-building certifications versus health and comfort standards, discuss the availability and reliability of air-quality monitors, and compare ventilation practices across regions and eras.
Tags: indoor air quality, building design, ventilation, HVAC, air-quality sensors, green buildings

## Relevance

The discussion includes a resident’s indoor CO2 reading, ventilation and filtration experiences, air-quality monitor reliability, and tradeoffs in airtight or energy-efficient buildings. It also contains conflicting claims about CO2 thresholds and building comfort. ## Arguments

- strict9 [[./thread#^c33316723|strict9]] argues that tighter buildings, smaller windows, and layouts maximizing floor area can reduce natural ventilation and retain indoor pollution. Gas ranges and ovens with little or no ventilation are their concrete exposure concern. This is a design argument; the comment supplies no cooking measurements, pollutant concentrations, or health outcomes.
- cameron4 [[./thread#^c33316778|cameron4]] advocates health-oriented construction standards, customer pressure, healthier materials, and passive ventilation. Foreignborn [[./thread#^c33316493|Foreignborn]], replying to a recollection of earlier sick-building concerns, quotes the article's account of litigation prompting materials and HVAC code changes in the 1990s. That historical improvement claim comes from an article quotation, not firsthand monitoring or a study presented in the discussion.
- walterbell [[./thread#^c33316863|walterbell]] recommends examining sensor manufacturers' specifications through DIY designs and highlights inadequate calibration as a consumer-sensor problem. They link to PM-sensor comparisons against a more expensive reference instrument, but provide no comparison results here. This supports checking measurement quality without establishing which device is accurate.
- dabeeeenster [[./thread#^c33316061|dabeeeenster]] proposes bringing a portable CO2 monitor when evaluating housing, responding directly to philip1209's [[./thread#^c33316048|philip1209]] difficulty screening a high-rise before moving in. It is a practical suggestion, not a reported successful screening experience; the thread does not establish whether a brief visit captures normal occupied conditions.

## Counterarguments

- philwelch [[./thread#^c33318277|philwelch]] disputes philip1209's [[./thread#^c33316048|philip1209]] characterization of roughly 1,000 ppm CO2 as high. They invoke a claimed OSHA maximum-safe level of 5,000 ppm and claim that ISS and nuclear-submarine crews routinely function at approximately that concentration without degraded performance. No supporting measurements or cognitive-test results are supplied, so these remain asserted counterarguments.
- Replying to cameron4's certification proposal, tgsovlerkhgsel [[./thread#^c33318056|tgsovlerkhgsel]] argues that environmental certification can conceal tradeoffs involving fresh air, mold, and comfort. They favor separate environmental and health/comfort ratings, while acknowledging that some certified buildings are pleasant. Their objection concerns the reliability of certification as a health signal, not a claim that every certified building is unhealthy.
- tgsovlerkhgsel [[./thread#^c33317881|tgsovlerkhgsel]] reports appreciating better perceived air quality in HVAC-equipped US accommodation compared with European residential settings relying mainly on windows. Conversely, kome [[./thread#^c33316273|kome]] says household AC is uncommon in Italy and guesses that this is beneficial. The former is a subjective travel comparison and the latter an opinion; neither establishes the ventilation or filtration performance of the systems involved.
- ChrisMarshallNY [[./thread#^c33316554|ChrisMarshallNY]] acknowledges that sick-building problems were real but recalls coworkers reporting attributed symptoms only after press coverage increased, interpreting some reaction as hysteria. This challenges uncomplicated attribution of every symptom to building exposure. The recollection cannot determine whether publicity changed symptom awareness, reporting, or underlying illness.

None provides comparative validation supporting a general rule about consumer versus commercial devices or price versus accuracy.
- kkfx [[./thread#^c33319690|kkfx]] treats visible differences on skin and clothing between cities and nature as evidence that household fine-particle filtration has limited reach. Such observations do not quantify PM2.5 exposure or filter effectiveness. Their description of electric filtration as the maximum available home technology is also an unsupported assertion within this source.
- When donutshop [[./thread#^c33315981|donutshop]] flags the absence of radon discussion, dusted [[./thread#^c33316141|dusted]] infers from the amount of regulation that radon has long been handled correctly. The exchange supplies no radon measurements, mitigation outcomes, or compliance evidence establishing that inference.

## Conditions

- cameron4 [[./thread#^c33316778|cameron4]] identifies construction budgets as a barrier: materials they describe as healthier are usually more expensive and are early targets for cost cutting. Their proposed improvements therefore depend partly on what clients will fund and retain during design and construction.
- cameron4 [[./thread#^c33316778|cameron4]] describes an American industry accustomed to HVAC-centered design, with limited passive-ventilation expertise among educators and project participants. Their firsthand basis includes graduating in 2020 after barely studying passive ventilation; the broader claims about the industry remain their assessment.
- ChrisMarshallNY [[./thread#^c33316349|ChrisMarshallNY]] explicitly attributes their ability to keep windows and screen doors open far into the seasons to a fairly temperate climate. kkfx [[./thread#^c33319690|kkfx]] cautions that outdoor conditions, including in nature, are not ideal throughout the year. Window-based ventilation is consequently presented as dependent on place and season, without a measured threshold for when it becomes beneficial or harmful.
- kkfx [[./thread#^c33319690|kkfx]] reports improved humidity and temperature control in a newer home but emphasizes the energy required. Their claim that these controls work well with renewables mainly on sunny days concerns the practical energy cost of indoor comfort; no consumption figures or system details are supplied.
- muro [[./thread#^c33317075|muro]] describes the Aranet4 as costing about three times as much as the CO2Meter RAD-0301 and wonders whether the premium mainly buys battery capability. This qualifies dabeeeenster's [[./thread#^c33316061|dabeeeenster]] assertion that portable monitors are inexpensive: affordability and the value of a premium remain user-dependent and untested in this discussion.

## Lived experiences

- philip1209 [[./thread#^c33316048|philip1209]] reports keeping an air-quality monitor in a high-rise bedroom and observing approximately 1,000 ppm CO2, which they describe as sustained internally. They consider the level concerning and say building engineering does not regard it as a problem. The account gives no sensor model, calibration, outdoor baseline, occupancy information, or associated symptoms; bedroom monitoring also does not establish conditions throughout the building.
- After moving from a concrete European home to a newer wood-frame home, kkfx [[./thread#^c33319690|kkfx]] reports noticing the absence of mechanical ventilation in other homes, more dust in wood-based housing, and better humidity and temperature control. These are perceived differences after a move, not measured PM2.5 changes or evidence isolating construction material as the cause.
- rygine [[./thread#^c33319834|rygine]] says an Airthings View Plus works well for them and that its app alerts them when readings exceed thresholds. They describe seven sensors and explicitly note the absence of CO measurement. This establishes a positive usability experience, without demonstrating accuracy, completeness, or exposure reduction.
- tgsovlerkhgsel [[./thread#^c33318056|tgsovlerkhgsel]] reports experiencing both pleasant buildings meeting an unpopular environmental standard and certified buildings whose certification-driven measures reduced comfort. Their separate US-versus-Europe air-quality impression [[./thread#^c33317881|tgsovlerkhgsel]] is also experiential. Neither account includes pollutant readings or a controlled comparison between otherwise similar buildings.
- ChrisMarshallNY [[./thread#^c33316349|ChrisMarshallNY]] reports routinely opening windows and screen doors, but gives no before-and-after readings or resulting health or sleep changes. In the ensuing historical discussion, ChrisMarshallNY [[./thread#^c33316554|ChrisMarshallNY]] recalls HEPA-industry growth and relays plumbers' and HVAC technicians' accounts of Legionnaires' disease precautions, including practices involving water, sunlight, and air. Those professional-practice details are secondhand and do not establish effectiveness.
