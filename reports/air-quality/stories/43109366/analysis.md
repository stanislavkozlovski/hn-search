# [Scented products cause indoor air pollution on par with car exhaust](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43109366)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

The discussion questions whether the headline fairly represents research on particles formed by scented products, distinguishing measured emissions from established health outcomes. Participants debate how to describe chemicals and natural versus synthetic substances, while sharing experiences with fragrance sensitivity, allergies, asthma, headaches, and scented products at home or work. Related threads cover indoor ventilation and filtration, particle sensors, cooking and combustion as pollution sources, and disagreement over the energy and resource costs of modern ventilation systems.
Tags: indoor air pollution, scented products, health research, fragrance sensitivity, particulate matter, ventilation, energy use

## Relevance

The discussion addresses indoor particles from scented products, reported allergy, asthma, and headache symptoms, and questions whether particle measurements imply comparable health effects. It also includes experiences with smoke, ventilation, and filtration, while emphasizing that the evidence and pollutant comparisons have limits. ## Arguments

- Combustion-free fragrance products can still generate airborne particles. KennyBlanken [[./thread#^c43111762|KennyBlanken]] [[./thread#^c43111780|KennyBlanken]] describes the research as comparing wax melts with scented candles by particle number, composition, size, and formation process. Clamchop [[./thread#^c43121720|Clamchop]] explains the proposed mechanism as terpenes reacting with ozone. These are commenters’ descriptions of research, supporting concern about emissions without establishing equivalent health risks across products.
- Removing an odor source can reduce the perceived need for added fragrance. TazeTSchnitzel [[./thread#^c43113022|TazeTSchnitzel]] suggests dishwasher maintenance when calgoo reports using a scented product to conceal smells; calgoo [[./thread#^c43114414|calgoo]] confirms cleaning was sufficient. wyclif [[./thread#^c43114382|wyclif]] recommends cleaning dust and fabrics and avoiding scented products. These practical suggestions have more direct support for odor control than for quantified reductions in pollutant exposure.
- Particle filtration and ventilation address different problems. energy123 [[./thread#^c43111390|energy123]] argues that ordinary purifiers can remove PM2.5 while providing little VOC removal and leaving CO2 accumulation unresolved. amluto [[./thread#^c43115854|amluto]] advocates deliberately supplied, filtered outdoor air, warning that negative pressure can draw air through paths that bypass filtration. Together these comments support combining source control, filtration, and air exchange according to the pollutant involved.
- Heat recovery is presented as a way to maintain fresh-air delivery while limiting thermal discomfort and heating demand. amluto [[./thread#^c43117109|amluto]] calculates that 100 cfm with a 30°F indoor–outdoor difference would require roughly 951 W of sensible heating without recovery, versus about 95 W plus 20–40 W of fan power with approximately 90\% recovery. This is an illustrative engineering calculation, separate from a measured household health benefit.
- Periodic airing is advocated for indoor odors and pollutants. wyclif [[./thread#^c43114382|wyclif]] recommends occasionally opening windows even in winter, while hinkley [[./thread#^c43116622|hinkley]] recommends airing during vacuuming because some disturbed dust escapes capture. Their advice provides no measurements of removal rate or resulting exposure; its applicability depends on the outdoor conditions described elsewhere in the discussion.
- Cooking and other combustion sources deserve attention alongside fragrance products. imp0cat [[./thread#^c43111685|imp0cat]] claims cooking causes the greatest indoor pollution, although no measurements accompany that ranking. Cthulhu\_ [[./thread#^c43115603|Cthulhu\_]] describes neighborhood wood fires producing lingering, noxious smoke. These comments identify plausible exposure sources without establishing their relative contributions in a particular home.
- Ventilation must be tolerable enough to remain switched on. Responding to Cthulhu\_’s account of residents disabling noisy systems [[./thread#^c43115568|Cthulhu\_]], amluto [[./thread#^c43116661|amluto]] recommends locating fans away from occupied rooms, using insulated flexible duct, and keeping airflow velocities low. The cited duct attenuation specification supports a proposed noise-control approach; no completed installation or before-and-after noise measurement is reported in that exchange.

## Counterarguments

- Similar particle emissions do not establish similar mortality or overall health risk. jmward01 [[./thread#^c43109986|jmward01]] objects specifically to the car-exhaust headline’s implied health comparison. KennyBlanken [[./thread#^c43111762|KennyBlanken]] responds that establishing particle formation is a legitimate research objective and that the paper calls for subsequent health research. Their disagreement concerns both how a headline communicates risk and what evidence is necessary to justify that communication.
- An exhaust comparison can omit consequential differences between pollutant mixtures. drusenko [[./thread#^c43112090|drusenko]] explicitly accepts concern about scented-product emissions while emphasizing combustion’s additional hazards, including CO, or carbon monoxide. timewizard [[./thread#^c43120391|timewizard]] instead assumes readers mainly understand pollution as particles and VOCs. This dispute over the comparison’s scope does not demonstrate that scented products are harmless.
- Smelling something does not itself demonstrate aerosol nanoparticles. seabass-labrax [[./thread#^c43109936|seabass-labrax]] and Twirrim [[./thread#^c43115055|Twirrim]] treat particle production as obvious from odor, but Clamchop [[./thread#^c43121720|Clamchop]] distinguishes gaseous odor compounds from particles formed through subsequent reactions. pama [[./thread#^c43110908|pama]] also distinguishes the technical meaning of nanoparticle from individual small gas molecules. This correction matters when interpreting both fragrance emissions and particle-sensor readings.
- Airtight construction need not mean stale air. amluto [[./thread#^c43115854|amluto]] and lm28469 [[./thread#^c43115417|lm28469]] describe sealed buildings supplied with filtered ventilation. macNchz [[./thread#^c43116731|macNchz]], however, describes apparently tight existing houses with little deliberate ventilation. These accounts concern different building configurations, so neither airtightness alone nor a building’s age establishes its indoor air quality.
- Natural outdoor sources are not uniformly comfortable or harmless. shellfishgene [[./thread#^c43113076|shellfishgene]] points to pollen, spores, and VOCs in forests; zemvpferreira [[./thread#^c43116767|zemvpferreira]] reports allergies to those natural exposures. jjk166 [[./thread#^c43117036|jjk166]] challenges the argument that evolutionary familiarity guarantees safety. benlivengood [[./thread#^c43121993|benlivengood]] similarly rejects natural wildfire smoke and pollen as desirable things to inhale.
- Claims that air quality has universally deteriorated meet a historical counterargument. Against asdff’s concern about modern sealed homes and synthetic materials [[./thread#^c43110361|asdff]], mlsu [[./thread#^c43110777|mlsu]] cites past exposure to leaded gasoline, coal heating, and tobacco smoke. asdff [[./thread#^c43121145|asdff]] replies using a much longer, preindustrial baseline. No comparable exposure series is supplied, and the participants are discussing partly different pollutants and periods.
- Comfort preferences conflict. anal\_reactor [[./thread#^c43112509|anal\_reactor]] reports weak smell sensitivity, enjoyment of strong scents, and substantial discomfort from winter window opening. Lanolderen [[./thread#^c43112508|Lanolderen]] enjoys a timed fragrance diffuser despite acknowledging possible risk. jjk166 [[./thread#^c43127270|jjk166]] prefers perfume-heavy odor masking around a litter box. These preferences complicate blanket prescriptions, while supplying no evidence that the exposures are safe.
- An incense-triggered purifier alarm is not shown to be a sensor failure. chakintosh [[./thread#^c43115666|chakintosh]] wonders whether the device mistakenly treats harmless particles as harmful; preinheimer [[./thread#^c43115739|preinheimer]] and mrtesthah [[./thread#^c43115736|mrtesthah]] challenge the unsupported premise that incense soot is harmless. The exchange distinguishes a device responding to emissions from a device determining their toxicological significance.

## Assumptions

- A meaningful source comparison requires comparable exposure conditions. drusenko [[./thread#^c43109901|drusenko]] questions whether candle, stove, generator, and exhaust comparisons use consistent durations and conditions, noting apparent reliance on other studies. The supplied discussion does not resolve those questions or establish that particle number, particle mass, chemical composition, and health burden are interchangeable measures.
- The word chemicals carries incompatible assumptions. 7thaccount [[./thread#^c43122362|7thaccount]] clarifies that they meant synthetic substances; schiffern [[./thread#^c43111899|schiffern]] interprets the shorthand as novel, untested synthetic substances; lukeschlather [[./thread#^c43115949|lukeschlather]] instead emphasizes substances already known to be harmful. MyOutfitIsVague [[./thread#^c43111667|MyOutfitIsVague]] objects that all air consists of chemicals. These meanings cannot support a single, well-defined exposure or safety category.
- Sensory reactions and material damage are incomplete hazard indicators. laborcontract [[./thread#^c43110849|laborcontract]] offers avoiding substances that provoke bodily reactions as a personal heuristic, then makes a broader, unsupported claim about smell detecting internal harm. ako’s report of leaked air freshener stripping paint [[./thread#^c43113395|ako]] demonstrates a contact effect on paint, without quantifying inhalation risk. Neither observation supplies a general toxicity ranking.
- Edibility is assumed to transfer across exposure routes in one exchange. MrMcCall [[./thread#^c43110492|MrMcCall]] proposes avoiding inhaled or skin-applied substances one would not ingest; sethammons [[./thread#^c43111991|sethammons]] responds that essential-oil products are edible. The discussion provides no basis for the broad edibility claim or for inferring inhalation safety from oral use.
- An unscented smell is assumed by some participants to mean no added fragrance. alabastervlog [[./thread#^c43114834|alabastervlog]] and Clamchop [[./thread#^c43121720|Clamchop]] claim that some products use fragrance to mask ingredient odors. Their comments raise a product-label interpretation issue, but provide no ingredient analysis of particular products or measurements of resulting exposure.
- Low perceived exposure is sometimes treated as sufficient reassurance. Lanolderen [[./thread#^c43112508|Lanolderen]] reasons that occasional household fragrance must be minor compared with vaping because vapers do not immediately die in large numbers. No comparable doses, chemical mixtures, or long-term outcomes are supplied to substantiate that relative-risk judgment.
- Large daily air intake is used to motivate concern, but it does not measure pollutant dose. xnx [[./thread#^c43109665|xnx]] gives approximately 20 pounds of inhaled air daily; moralestapia [[./thread#^c43111561|moralestapia]] revises a much larger initial estimate to roughly 13–14 kg after reconsidering breath volume. These are calculations and estimates, not personal pollution measurements or evidence of a particular health effect.

## Conditions

- Opening windows depends on the air available outside that particular opening. MrMcCall [[./thread#^c43110472|MrMcCall]] keeps windows closed during rush hour and considers wind direction. exe34 [[./thread#^c43115564|exe34]] reports that opening a window to escape a neighbor’s cooking and incense admits the neighbor’s cigarette smoke. Cthulhu\_ [[./thread#^c43115603|Cthulhu\_]] describes weather that traps neighborhood fire smoke. These accounts support location- and timing-dependent ventilation decisions rather than a universal open-window rule.
- Climate and building fabric limit comparisons between naturally ventilated homes and insulated mechanical systems. alt227 [[./thread#^c43112030|alt227]] [[./thread#^c43115738|alt227]] describes a comfortable old UK stone house and local extremes from approximately −10°C to the mid-30s. nkrisc [[./thread#^c43113259|nkrisc]] describes Lake Michigan extremes down to −28°C and values modern heating and insulation. amluto [[./thread#^c43116898|amluto]] cautions that stone’s thermal mass and underground temperature stability should not be confused with good wall insulation.
- Ventilation availability varies with region, construction era, and builder. erikerikson [[./thread#^c43111262|erikerikson]] and joemazerino [[./thread#^c43110632|joemazerino]] broadly associate sealed modern homes with HRV/ERV systems. hypercube33 [[./thread#^c43111365|hypercube33]] disputes that expectation for Midwest contractors, while macNchz [[./thread#^c43116731|macNchz]] describes US housing from roughly the 1970s through the mid-2000s as potentially tight but poorly ventilated. These are observations and impressions, not a verified survey of buildings or codes.
- Money and control over housing constrain mitigation. energy123 [[./thread#^c43111390|energy123]] considers substantial gas filtration, filtered ventilation, or relocation difficult to afford, with work limiting where people can move. terribleperson [[./thread#^c43112002|terribleperson]] highlights carbon filters as an additional consumable. MrMcCall [[./thread#^c43110472|MrMcCall]] reports paying for a costly purifier while still encountering other users’ scented-product residues in a shared laundromat.
- Installation space and ongoing replacement costs matter. amluto [[./thread#^c43118275|amluto]] describes a physically large 24-by-24-by-12-inch HEPA filter, a MERV 8 prefilter, and a carbon mat replacing an undersized factory filter. They expect years of HEPA service, roughly annual prefilter replacement, and more frequent carbon replacement if wildfire odors become troublesome. These are expected lifetimes, with no long-term replacement record supplied.
- Local power reliability and heating arrangements affect the ventilation tradeoff. alt227 [[./thread#^c43118713|alt227]] values a home reportedly heated and ventilated without grid electricity and unaffected by frequent outages. amluto [[./thread#^c43118275|amluto]] evaluates fan electricity against avoided heat loss and filtration demands. lm28469 [[./thread#^c43148242|lm28469]] notes that windows remain available during outages. Their preferred systems depend on different household circumstances and accounting boundaries.
- Outdoor smoke can reflect local fuel economics as well as weather. Cthulhu\_ [[./thread#^c43115603|Cthulhu\_]] reports that expensive firewood encourages neighbors to burn whatever materials they can obtain, sometimes with poor equipment or technique. Separately, asdff [[./thread#^c43121267|asdff]] argues that regional lumber availability, seismic risk, and ground conditions affect the cost and practicality of copying an old European stone-house design. Neither account supplies quantified costs or exposure comparisons.

## Lived experiences

- 7thaccount [[./thread#^c43110051|7thaccount]] reports severe allergies and asthma and finds many fragranced household and personal products troublesome. Their rideshare follow-up [[./thread#^c43122391|7thaccount]] describes anticipating sneezing, headaches, and wheezing around air fresheners. readthenotes1 [[./thread#^c43110750|readthenotes1]] reports nasal blockage, watery eyes, and sneezing around scents, with bloomingeek [[./thread#^c43111450|bloomingeek]] identifying similar reactions to incense and many home fireplaces. These are individual symptom reports, without pollutant concentrations.
- kstrauser [[./thread#^c43111825|kstrauser]] reports sinus swelling and chest tightening during unannounced workplace insecticide spraying, noticing symptoms before discovering the spraying. The account also describes a prior request for advance warning being disregarded. It supports the importance of taking reported sensitivities seriously while leaving the chemical, dose, and clinical mechanism unspecified.
- neilv [[./thread#^c43111001|neilv]] reports that headaches while folding laundry stopped immediately after discontinuing dryer sheets and scented detergent. They separately report that complaints of headaches and breathing problems among people and pets near a condo building’s laundry exhaust subsided after most residents stopped scented laundry products. The personal before-and-after observation is distinct from their account of others’ complaints; neither includes controlled measurements.
- exe34 [[./thread#^c43115564|exe34]] reports nausea from a downstairs neighbor’s burned garlic and incense entering through the floor, followed by cigarette smoke entering the window opened for relief. Their follow-ups describe an impending move and concern about recovering a deposit [[./thread#^c43117581|exe34]], while acknowledging unusually high sensitivity [[./thread#^c43118163|exe34]]. The account illustrates an exposure problem that cannot be solved simply by opening a window.
- calgoo [[./thread#^c43112513|calgoo]] reports persistent dishwasher-fragrance odors in travel mugs and being sprayed in the face by automatic fragrance dispensers. The household initially disagreed about whether the scent was objectionable. calgoo’s later confirmation that cleaning solved the dishwasher’s original odor [[./thread#^c43114414|calgoo]] gives a concrete maintenance outcome, without demonstrating a change in airborne pollutant concentration.
- culopatin [[./thread#^c43117058|culopatin]] reports headaches triggered by particular orange scents and diesel exhaust, while reporting no comparable symptoms from brake cleaner or race fuel. theodric [[./thread#^c43109960|theodric]] describes some fragrances as distracting or headache-inducing, and intreble [[./thread#^c43110466|intreble]] finds certain artificial-seeming scent combinations especially unpleasant. These accounts show selective responses; lack of immediate symptoms does not establish the safety of other exposures.
- an\_aparallel [[./thread#^c43119306|an\_aparallel]] describes powerful perfumes in Sydney lunchrooms and streets as making some days unbearable, tentatively attributing them to ambroxan. The ingredient identification is uncertain, and no concentration or medical outcome is reported.
- TylerE [[./thread#^c43110760|TylerE]] reports that wearing an N95 reduces many common scents, including rideshare air fresheners, and suggests a carbon-containing N100 would be preferable. This is a subjective experience of odor reduction. No mask testing, fit information, or measurements separating particle capture from vapor removal are provided.
- javier\_e06 [[./thread#^c43116824|javier\_e06]] bought a multi-function air-quality sensor during Canadian wildfire events. They report a current reading identified as CO2 of 548 ppm, which they regard as good, and a separate scented-candle episode exceeding 1,200 ppm. The current value is not explicitly the candle experiment’s baseline. Their suggestion that a wooden wick explains the increase remains speculative.
- chakintosh [[./thread#^c43115666|chakintosh]] reports that lighting incense made a purifier display red and immediately run its fan at maximum speed. This is an instrument response associated with a source event, but the comment gives no numerical concentration, particle-size channel, sensor model, or calibration information.
- instagib [[./thread#^c43111996|instagib]], writing in verse, reports that a roughly 30-minute candle burn separated by a door and a floor was followed by two sooty filters and a campfire-like taste through a CPAP despite air handlers and purifiers. The account suggests perceived transport of combustion products within that home; it supplies no exposure measurement or sleep outcome.
- amluto [[./thread#^c43118275|amluto]] reports operating an ERV at 25 W with a slightly positive-pressure, HEPA-filtered supply and enlarged aftermarket filters. They report lower filter resistance than the factory arrangement. The accompanying 500 W heat-loss comparison and estimated need for at least 100 W of standalone purifiers describe alternatives, rather than a documented side-by-side experiment. No indoor particle concentrations are supplied.
- Cthulhu\_ [[./thread#^c43115568|Cthulhu\_]] describes their own insulated home with exhaust ventilation drawing in outdoor air. Their account of nearby residents turning down or disabling noisy heat-exchanger systems, followed by health problems attributed to CO2 and other buildup, concerns other households. No CO2 readings, diagnoses, or causal investigation are reported.
- Thermal comfort accounts differ across homes. alt227 [[./thread#^c43112030|alt227]] reports fresh air without drafts, dampness, or uncomfortable temperatures in a 300-year-old UK house. lm28469 [[./thread#^c43148216|lm28469]] recalls feeling cold in a Florida home at 15°C outside but managing without heating in Germany until approximately 0°C. nkrisc [[./thread#^c43113259|nkrisc]] recalls a cold week in a recreated dwelling heated by indoor fires, with everything smelling of smoke. These experiences do not establish comparative pollutant concentrations.
- 14 [[./thread#^c43111704|14]] reports that a workplace scent policy and a boss willing to request client accommodation reduce unwanted exposures, while strong fragrances used by their teenagers remain difficult to tolerate. The account identifies organizational source control as a practical intervention and shows that exposure control varies across workplaces, clients’ homes, and family settings.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The magnitude of chronic harm from these particular fragrance emissions remains unresolved. jmward01 [[./thread#^c43109986|jmward01]] wants health-outcome evidence before making a car-exhaust comparison; KennyBlanken [[./thread#^c43111762|KennyBlanken]] maintains that particle characterization is valuable research and defends accessible headline framing. The supplied comments do not establish a dose–response relationship, comparable mortality risk, or a safe duration of use.
- Sensor validity and quantitative particulate exposure remain open. javier\_e06 [[./thread#^c43116824|javier\_e06]] provides ppm readings described as CO2, without identifying whether the sensor measures CO2 directly or estimates it, its calibration, or the room conditions. chakintosh [[./thread#^c43115666|chakintosh]] provides a purifier indicator response. Neither supplies numerical PM2.5 concentrations, and the discussion does not establish that either device is accurate or faulty.
- This source unit supplies no NO2 measurements and no measured cognitive or sleep outcomes. drusenko [[./thread#^c43109901|drusenko]] discusses combustion and CO; energy123 [[./thread#^c43111390|energy123]] speculates that CO2 can acutely impair cognition below 1,000 ppm without supplying supporting results; instagib [[./thread#^c43111996|instagib]] describes CPAP odor and soot. These comments cannot establish pollutant-specific NO2 effects or the size of any cognitive or sleep benefit from mitigation.
- Wildfire protection and gas-filter performance are not quantified. javier\_e06 [[./thread#^c43116824|javier\_e06]] mentions wildfire events as the reason for purchasing a sensor, while amluto [[./thread#^c43118275|amluto]] anticipates replacing a carbon mat more frequently if wildfire odors become a problem. energy123 [[./thread#^c43112031|energy123]] argues that thin carbon sheets are insufficient and substantial carbon is required. No smoke PM2.5 measurements, gas-removal tests, or carbon service-life measurements resolve the practical performance questions.
- Houseplant benefits remain contested, with participants discussing different endpoints. dkdbejwi383 [[./thread#^c43114417|dkdbejwi383]] says useful purification requires impractically many plants; marcosdumay [[./thread#^c43115060|marcosdumay]] emphasizes particles, humidity, and plants’ own emissions. Retric [[./thread#^c43118765|Retric]] claims meaningful daytime CO2 reductions but nighttime increases. wyclif [[./thread#^c43125797|wyclif]] moderates the original claim. No household measurements establish the magnitude of any of these effects.
- The natural-versus-mechanical ventilation debate never establishes a shared baseline. alt227 [[./thread#^c43118713|alt227]] emphasizes freedom from grid electricity and outages; amluto [[./thread#^c43118275|amluto]] emphasizes filtered supply and avoided heat loss. mapleoin’s request for the old house’s actual indoor winter temperature [[./thread#^c43112907|mapleoin]] remains unanswered. Without comparable temperatures, ventilation rates, outdoor pollution, and heating inputs, the discussion cannot determine which arrangement would perform better elsewhere.
- Relative risks across fragrance devices remain unanswered. porphyra [[./thread#^c43109695|porphyra]] asks about essential-oil diffusers versus smoking, printers, and tire emissions; kylehotchkiss [[./thread#^c43117726|kylehotchkiss]] asks whether ultrasonic diffusion in water reduces toxicity or whether wax is central. toddmorey [[./thread#^c43109896|toddmorey]] raises hotel fragrance systems. None receives a measured comparison, so the wax-melt discussion cannot establish a risk ranking across these exposure sources.
