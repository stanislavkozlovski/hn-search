# [Gas stoves became part of America’s culture wars](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34504565)

## Description

Discussion of gas stoves and culture-war politics turns on the strength of evidence linking indoor gas use with childhood asthma and what policy should follow. Participants dispute whether the evidence demonstrates causation, ask for scholarly sources, and argue over the difference between correlation and proof. Some favor managing risks through ventilation standards and better indoor air quality, while others question broad safety restrictions or suggest the debate is connected to efforts to phase out household natural gas. The thread reflects both scientific disagreement and political polarization.
Tags: gas stoves, asthma, indoor air quality, public health, regulation, energy politics

## Relevance

The discussion weighs claims about gas stoves and childhood asthma against uncertainty about causation, and considers ventilation and filtration as practical risk controls. ## Arguments

- qikInNdOutReply [[./thread#^c34506161|qikInNdOutReply]] argues that gas stoves cause childhood asthma and should therefore be discontinued for household use. They cite a linked publication, but the supplied comment contains no study methods, exposure measurements, or effect sizes; its claim of scientific proof cannot be verified from this export.
- some0x80070005 [[./thread#^c34506537|some0x80070005]], replying directly to that argument, proposes managing exposure through mandatory fume hoods for new gas-stove installations, with specified airflow and CO2 or particulate sensors. prottog [[./thread#^c34506261|prottog]] separately advocates filtration and ventilation, saying the original linked source recommends these options. Both offer mitigation proposals rather than measured intervention results.

## Counterarguments

- tptacek [[./thread#^c34506199|tptacek]] [[./thread#^c34506379|tptacek]] disputes the claim of proven causation, calls causation somewhat unlikely, and says any effect appears insignificant. Their follow-up links an Emily Oster article and a social-media thread, but supplies no numerical findings. The objection to certainty and the stronger assertion that an effect is unlikely should be distinguished.
- CraigJPerry [[./thread#^c34506294|CraigJPerry]], replying to tptacek, points to what they describe as a meta-analysis of 41 studies and requests evidence for the claim that causation is unlikely. malfist [[./thread#^c34506276|malfist]] independently requests a scholarly countercitation, suggesting a study finding no correlation. These replies challenge the support for dismissal; neither reproduces findings that settle causation.
- prottog [[./thread#^c34506261|prottog]] says the original link describes correlation rather than causation and challenges the inference that a ban must follow. some0x80070005 [[./thread#^c34506537|some0x80070005]] similarly argues that hazards can be managed, invoking building codes and laboratory fume hoods. Those analogies support a risk-management position but do not demonstrate that household controls achieve comparable protection.

## Assumptions

- qikInNdOutReply [[./thread#^c34506161|qikInNdOutReply]] treats the asserted childhood-asthma risk as sufficient grounds to end household gas-stove use. some0x80070005 [[./thread#^c34506537|some0x80070005]] and prottog [[./thread#^c34506261|prottog]] instead presume an acceptable residual risk could be achieved through controls. No shared acceptable-risk threshold or comparison of intervention benefits and burdens is supplied.

The comments do not demonstrate that these measures address the same pollutants equally.
