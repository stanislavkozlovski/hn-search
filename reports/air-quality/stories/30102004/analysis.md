# [Americans’ Gas Stoves Are as Bad for Climate as 500k Cars](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=30102004)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Commenters debate the climate impact of residential gas stoves and whether electrification should prioritize cooking, heating, or transportation. They compare gas and electric operating costs, induction efficiency, installation expenses, indoor air quality, ventilation, and grid reliability, including concerns about power outages and emissions from electricity generation. The discussion challenges the headline’s comparison with cars and considers methane leaks, fixed gas-connection fees, appliance replacement, and whether incentives or infrastructure changes are more effective than immediate switching.
Tags: gas-stoves, climate-emissions, electrification, induction, indoor-air-quality, energy-costs

## Relevance

Despite the climate-focused title, comments directly address indoor pollution from gas stoves, the health value of ventilation, and tradeoffs in switching to electric cooking. They debate whether range hoods remove pollutants effectively and whether tighter homes need more fresh-air ventilation. ## Arguments

- bushbaba [[./thread#^c30104502|bushbaba]] argues that poorly ventilated gas-stove fumes can pose health risks and that switching to induction might improve adults’ and children’s health. This is a proposed benefit, without reported exposure measurements or health outcomes.
- floatingatoll [[./thread#^c30104257|floatingatoll]] argues for addressing stove emissions together with inadequate fresh-air inflow and exhaust. This frames indoor exposure as a combination of the pollution source and ventilation, although the comment supplies no airflow measurements or effectiveness estimates.
- tssva [[./thread#^c30109438|tssva]] distinguishes ductless hoods, which filter and recirculate air, from removal through exhaust. They suspect recirculation does little to remove the offending substances; this is explicitly conjecture, without identifying pollutants or testing filters.

## Counterarguments

- Replying directly to the concern about recirculating hoods, beerandt [[./thread#^c30116355|beerandt]] claims mixing and dilution safely mitigate most natural-gas malfunctions and invokes ductless hoods’ code compliance. The argument concerns concentrated gas buildup and malfunctions; it provides no measurements demonstrating control of routine cooking emissions.
- Responding to barbazoo’s hope that ventilation will help [[./thread#^c30104858|barbazoo]], tchock23 [[./thread#^c30106436|tchock23]] recalls reading that most fume hoods lack sufficient power to remove the worst pollution, but cannot find the source. This challenges confidence in ordinary hood performance without establishing which installations or pollutants are affected.
- In a separate discussion about relying entirely on electricity, bradstewart [[./thread#^c30103066|bradstewart]] objects that independent gas service provided essential heating and cooking during a Texas power failure. This is a practical resilience concern about replacing gas appliances, with no comparison of indoor exposures.

## Assumptions

- The suggested health improvement from induction in bushbaba [[./thread#^c30104502|bushbaba]] assumes changing the stove’s fuel would meaningfully reduce the relevant exposure. The comment does not distinguish combustion emissions from other cooking byproducts or quantify how much pollution would remain.
- beerandt [[./thread#^c30116355|beerandt]] assumes dilution and avoidance of concentrated gas accumulation adequately address the concern raised about ductless hoods. No pollutant-specific concentration thresholds or health evidence establish that equivalence. Their further claim that tightly sealed homes worsen buildup, while older homes naturally ventilate, also lacks measurements here.
- Fuel-price comparisons depend on the efficiency boundary. tannranger [[./thread#^c30103611|tannranger]] explicitly excludes conversion inefficiencies. In an adjacent exchange, smileysteve [[./thread#^c30103417|smileysteve]] calls induction substantially more efficient than resistance heating; mikewarot [[./thread#^c30105404|mikewarot]] challenges this using resistance heating’s asserted 100\% efficiency; 8note [[./thread#^c30106479|8note]] and TheCoelacanth [[./thread#^c30109400|TheCoelacanth]] distinguish heating an element or the surroundings from heating the intended pan. No measured cooking-efficiency comparison resolves the practical magnitude.

## Conditions

- Conversion can require substantial installation work: Kon-Peki [[./thread#^c30102526|Kon-Peki]] says replacing gas with electric can cost twice the stove’s price once a 30, 40, or 50 amp circuit and capping or removing the gas line are considered. This is a general cost estimate, not an itemized personal installation bill.
- Reported January 2022 energy prices vary geographically. tannranger [[./thread#^c30103611|tannranger]] reports approximately \$2 per therm and \$0.30/kWh in California’s Bay Area, estimating gas at about \$0.07 per equivalent input kWh before efficiency differences. ZetaZero [[./thread#^c30103929|ZetaZero]] reports Georgia prices of \$0.0566/kWh for electricity and \$1.09 per therm, approximately \$0.038 per equivalent kWh, before additional fees. These local examples do not establish a universal operating-cost gap.
- Whether conversion eliminates the gas connection matters. throwaway0a5e [[./thread#^c30102589|throwaway0a5e]] emphasizes electricity’s higher cost per BTU, while conjecTech [[./thread#^c30103396|conjecTech]] counters that fixed connection charges dominate their bill because the range is their only gas appliance. Their projected savings depend on being able to discontinue gas service entirely.
- Cold-weather reliability complicates appliance choices. teeray [[./thread#^c30103622|teeray]] describes mixed propane, electric, or wood heating in northern New England as protection against frozen pipes. olliej [[./thread#^c30104899|olliej]] attributes Texas’s exceptional outage severity to state decisions and disputes treating it as representative of similarly cold states; bradstewart [[./thread#^c30106276|bradstewart]] accepts that qualification while maintaining the value of redundancy.

## Lived experiences

- barbazoo [[./thread#^c30102867|barbazoo]] says learning about the health implications of gas-cooking byproducts prompted an intention to ventilate more carefully while postponing stove replacement. Their follow-up [[./thread#^c30104858|barbazoo]] expresses hope for a short-term benefit; it reports no completed intervention, measurements, or symptom improvement.
- bradstewart [[./thread#^c30103066|bradstewart]] reports that uninterrupted gas service, combined with a small 1,500-watt generator powering furnace controls and the blower, let their household heat and cook during the Texas outage. This establishes an experienced service-continuity benefit, without indoor-temperature or air-quality readings. Their suggestion that solar and batteries could substitute remains conditional on uncertain runtime.
- conjecTech [[./thread#^c30103396|conjecTech]] reports an \$18 monthly gas connection fee against just \$0.73 of gas consumption charges in the preceding month. They estimate saving close to \$200 annually by becoming fully electric; those savings are prospective, not observed after conversion.
- Kon-Peki [[./thread#^c30103052|Kon-Peki]] describes moving from electric cooking in a condo to gas cooking in a house with the same household size and amount of cooking, but does not know the resulting savings. tannranger [[./thread#^c30104353|tannranger]] reports summer gas spending around 20\% of winter spending in a household using gas for space heating and hot water, and guesses stove use rarely costs more than \$10 monthly. Neither account isolates measured stove consumption.
