  Nitrogen oxide & dioxide (NOx) air pollution is certainly a problem, but in terms of policy and impact, diesel-burning engines are probably the greatest immediate problem. ^c34477584

  Fundamentally, the issue is that the atmosphere is 80\% N2 and when combustion takes place at high pressure and/or high temperature, some fraction of that N2 gets converted to NOx species.  Gas burns at a high enough temperature to drive this process, and internal combustion engines (especially higher-pressure diesel engines) do the same.

  Using gas-powered stoves and heaters can certainly increase indoor levels of NOx without a good ventilation and exhaust system.  Poorly adjusted gas burners of all kinds can also generate carbon monoxide, CO, due to incomplete combustion, which kills about 400 people a year in the USA and accounts for 100K hospital visits (CDC).

  However, diesel (and older gasoline) engines generate an additional list of air pollutants that greatly increase asthma and other respiratory ailments: ozone and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which undergo further reactions with NOx in the atmosphere to form things like PAN (peroxyacyl nitrates). This toxic cocktail makes up much of the smog that hovers over so many regions, and it definitely is a major cause of childhood asthma, certainly more so than indoor gas stoves.

  Hence in terms of regulatory approaches, replacing all diesel vehicles with electric ones would have a greater impact on childhood asthma than something like a ban on gas stoves would.  Ensuring homes with gas appliances have good exhaust and ventilation would make more immediate sense.

  (2018) Diesel, children and respiratory disease

  [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5976105/](<https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5976105/>)

    \> than something like a ban on gas stoves would

    The proposed bans concern bans on gas hookups for new housing, which seems relatively inexpensive and doesn't require the infrastructure changes of changing all diesel (and older gasoline) engines to battery.

    It's also easier to tell the builder they cannot get gas hookups than to shut of gas to a house which doesn't have the wiring to switch to electric stoves and heat.

    \> Poorly adjusted gas burners of ... kills about 400 people a year in the USA and accounts for 100K hospital visits.

    The CDC numbers I found give stronger support for reducing petroleum engines than for improving exhaust. Over half of those 400 accidental deaths due to CO poisoning come from automobile engines, and another quarter come from generators and engine-based tools.

    Less than a quarter (I couldn't find an accurate breakdown) come from poor ventilation of gas-powered stoves and heaters.

    The CDC numbers I found were for 50K hospital visits, and I didn't find a breakdown for how many come specifically from poor home ventilation.

    There certainly are some, and I support the proposal that HUD needs the funding to provide and maintain CO detectors for HUD subsidized housing, which would help.

    But I think the push for decreasing the number of gas stoves can be done on a different timescale, and in parallel, to the the push for decreasing the number of gas and diesel vehicles.

    And I think both can be placed primarily under the umbrella of reducing NOx exposure.

        There's also this comment from the second paper, which the Slate author didn't mention:

        \] It is still possible that the associations we found in this study with nitrogen dioxide could be attributable to unmeasured indoor air contaminants, such as particulates derived from gas appliances. This is supported by the fact that, in Menorca, the principal source of gas appliances is butane bottles, a fuel more toxic than natural gas (36)

        This makes it hard to compare butane cooking use in Spain with, say, natural gas cooking use in the US.

        Further, they didn't conclude that there was brain damage. They found a correlation with "cognitive functioning and inattention-hyperactivity symptoms in preschoolers" and conjectured it was due to nitrogen dioxide exposure causing brain cell damage, so saying "brain damage" is going beyond the evidence.

        To answer your question, the resources you pointed to suggest 1) that the amount of brain damage is small and not well established, 2) that other factors play a much larger role in comparing brain damage between North American and European countries (eg, lead), and 3) that the link between gas burning and asthma and respiratory problems is a much more relevant topic to focus on.
