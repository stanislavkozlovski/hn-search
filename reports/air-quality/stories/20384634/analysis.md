# [Indoor carbon dioxide levels could be a health hazard](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=20384634)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants discuss claims that indoor CO₂ can affect health and cognition, and whether ventilation or air conditioning changes indoor levels. They compare airflow needs, ask about affordable CO₂ monitors, and share experiences measuring levels in homes and offices. Some question how much CO₂ itself contributes to discomfort compared with heat and humidity.
Tags: indoor air quality, CO2, ventilation, air conditioning, sensors

## Arguments

- RandomWorker interprets a linked Nature article as strong evidence that indoor CO2 affects health and cognition, making monitoring a workplace productivity priority. They recommend avoiding prolonged levels above 700, apparently ppm. These are the commenter's interpretation and recommendation; the study's methods and results are not supplied [[./thread#^c20385541|RandomWorker]].
- QuickToBan recommends buying and using a CO2 meter [[./thread#^c20392388|QuickToBan]]. Rch\_East provides an example of monitoring changing behavior: after observing CO2 accumulation in a closed apartment, they began keeping a window open whenever home. They do not report a measured reduction or resulting health improvement [[./thread#^c20388835|Rch\_East]].

## Counterarguments

- southern\_cross challenges the emphasis on ambient CO2 by claiming normal lung concentrations are about 40,000 ppm and attributing crowded-room fatigue and discomfort to heat and humidity. Their reported relief from AC supports an alternative explanation for discomfort, but supplies neither an ambient exposure threshold nor a controlled comparison of causes [[./thread#^c20386411|southern\_cross]].
- The AC exchange distinguishes circulation from fresh-air ventilation. annamargot asks whether AC removes CO2 [[./thread#^c20387632|annamargot]], and rusticpenn argues that outdoor exhaust implies airflow [[./thread#^c20390538|rusticpenn]]. southern\_cross replies that heat rejection generally uses a separate airflow while indoor air recirculates [[./thread#^c20394807|southern\_cross]]. Their earlier reply allows that a multi-room system could redistribute CO2 into less occupied rooms, without actually removing it from the building [[./thread#^c20389546|southern\_cross]].

## Assumptions

- The proposed cognitive thresholds remain unestablished within the supplied text. RandomWorker's suggested ceiling of 700 is not accompanied by an exposure-response analysis [[./thread#^c20385541|RandomWorker]]. QuickToBan's prediction that everyone would suffer cognitive impairment if ambient CO2 reached 800 ppm is an extrapolation about inhaled air, rather than a firsthand observation or a demonstrated universal effect [[./thread#^c20392434|QuickToBan]].
- DoofusOfDeath's ventilation calculation assumes replacement air comes from elsewhere in the house. Its usefulness depends on that air's CO2 concentration, which is unspecified; the target concentration and calculation are also absent. The commenter explicitly doubts their arithmetic [[./thread#^c20385947|DoofusOfDeath]].
- Using the recommended monitors to guide behavior assumes adequate measurement accuracy. Rch\_East says their combined PM2.5/HCHO/TVOC/CO2 instrument works very well [[./thread#^c20388835|Rch\_East]], while QuickToBan says an Amazon-linked device works well [[./thread#^c20392397|QuickToBan]]. Neither endorsement includes calibration, sensing technology, accuracy specifications, or comparison against a reference instrument.

## Conditions

- Affordability is an explicit concern: spookybones requests a decent, affordable bedroom or small-office instrument [[./thread#^c20386473|spookybones]]. Rch\_East recommends a \$90 device they own [[./thread#^c20388835|Rch\_East]]. QuickToBan's alternative endorsement supplies no price, so the comments do not establish comparative value or accuracy per dollar [[./thread#^c20392397|QuickToBan]].
- southern\_cross describes a ventilation-versus-conditioning tradeoff: bringing in outside air could increase the work needed to cool and dehumidify a room, potentially requiring continuous AC operation. No electricity costs, local weather, or geographic conditions are supplied, limiting practical comparisons across homes and climates [[./thread#^c20394807|southern\_cross]].

## Lived experiences

- RandomWorker reports working with 13 people in slightly more than 20 m² and says the group is tired at the end of the day. They suspect excessive CO2, but report no measurements; the fatigue is firsthand testimony, while its explanation remains speculative [[./thread#^c20385541|RandomWorker]].
- southern\_cross reports feeling tired and uncomfortable in crowded rooms and feeling better shortly after AC reduces heat and humidity. This is a subjective comfort account without simultaneous CO2 readings or evidence separating temperature, humidity, and ventilation effects [[./thread#^c20386411|southern\_cross]].
- QuickToBan explicitly attributes their conclusion to measurement: AC use doubles or triples indoor CO2, which they explain as decreased ventilation. They supply no absolute concentrations, equipment description, timing, occupancy, or comparison conditions, so the observed increase cannot be generalized to all AC systems [[./thread#^c20392413|QuickToBan]].
- Rch\_East reports that their inexpensive 60 m² apartment exceeds 800 ppm after approximately two hours closed up with one person breathing inside. They own the recommended monitor and now keep a window open whenever home. There are no outdoor readings, post-opening measurements, or associated symptom reports [[./thread#^c20388835|Rch\_East]].
- While constructing a home office, DoofusOfDeath calculated ventilation for two occupants in 1,200 ft³, using replacement air from the house. They vaguely recall needing around 100 cfm, above the ASHRAE 62-89 recommendation they quote as at least 20 cfm per person. This is an uncertain design calculation, not a measured airflow requirement or a verified comparison with the standard [[./thread#^c20385947|DoofusOfDeath]].

## Unresolved disagreements

- The discussion does not resolve whether CO2 itself explains the reported fatigue or cognitive risk. RandomWorker emphasizes the linked research and suspects workplace exposure [[./thread#^c20385541|RandomWorker]]; southern\_cross emphasizes heat, humidity, and lung CO2 concentrations [[./thread#^c20386411|southern\_cross]]. No supplied account connects measured concentrations to tested cognition, diagnosed health effects, or improved sleep after an intervention.
- AC's effect remains dependent on undocumented circumstances. southern\_cross describes recirculation and possible redistribution between rooms [[./thread#^c20389546|southern\_cross]], whereas QuickToBan reports increased CO2 and attributes it to reduced ventilation [[./thread#^c20392413|QuickToBan]]. These accounts could coexist if AC operation coincided with less fresh-air exchange, but the comments do not establish the mechanism or its generality.
- The wider pollutant and window-opening questions remain unanswered in this source unit. PM2.5 appears only among Rch\_East's monitor capabilities; they provide no particle measurements or outdoor-air comparison [[./thread#^c20388835|Rch\_East]]. There are no supplied NO2 measurements, filtration trials, or cooking, traffic, or wildfire-smoke experiences. Their window-opening practice therefore cannot establish when outdoor exposure would outweigh any indoor CO2 benefit.
