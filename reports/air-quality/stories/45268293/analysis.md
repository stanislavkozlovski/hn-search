# [Frying Eggs and Air Quality Tests](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45268293)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

The discussion examines why frying eggs produced a modest particulate reading and whether the result generalizes to cooking with oil, higher heat, smoke, or browning. Participants share measurements from other kitchens and debate the contributions of oil aerosols, burning food, gas combustion, and particle composition. A major thread considers kitchen ventilation: externally vented hoods versus recirculating fans, airflow and makeup air, and how open layouts spread odors and pollutants. People also weigh enclosed kitchens against cooking as a social activity and discuss sensor limits, purifier responses, and the difference between particle readings and health risk.
Tags: cooking emissions, particulate matter, air-quality sensors, kitchen ventilation, range hoods, cooking oil, gas stoves, indoor air quality, open-plan kitchens, air purifiers

## Relevance

The discussion assesses firsthand PM2.5 readings during cooking and the differing effects of oil, smoke and burning food, while noting that particulate sensors do not reveal particle composition and may miss ultrafine particles. It also examines kitchen exhaust performance, recirculating versus externally vented hoods, makeup air and ventilation tradeoffs, alongside distinctions between particles from cooking and gases from gas combustion. ## Arguments

- Cooking itself can generate indoor particles independently of the stove's fuel. crazygringo attributes frying emissions to smoking oil and aerosolization below the smoke point [[./thread#^c45301367|crazygringo]]. The self-identified author, casualphysics, confirms using induction and oil [[./thread#^c45317170|casualphysics]]. This distinction matters when evaluating whether changing from gas to induction addresses all cooking-related exposure.
- Gas cooking raises questions that a particulate reading alone cannot answer. crazygringo names CO2, NO2 and some CO as combustion products [[./thread#^c45301367|crazygringo]]; mikeyouse names NOx, benzene and other VOCs [[./thread#^c45301571|mikeyouse]]. These are explanatory claims in this discussion, not reported gas measurements. adolph also argues that NOx formation concerns combustion in air, rather than simply the fuel's fossil origin [[./thread#^c45306511|adolph]].
- Several commenters favor reducing emissions at their source. groggler recommends avoiding high heat and using oil with a higher smoke point [[./thread#^c45307554|groggler]], while mushroomba reports changing cooking styles and moving certain preparations outside to avoid vaporizing fat [[./thread#^c45305439|mushroomba]]. These proposals are not accompanied by measured exposure reductions.
- Externally exhausting cooking emissions is preferred to returning them to the room. ssimpson contrasts outside exhaust with loosely filtered recirculation [[./thread#^c45301415|ssimpson]], and amo1111 explicitly agrees that properly implemented outside ventilation is superior to recirculation [[./thread#^c45302445|amo1111]]. Neither establishes how much protection a particular installed hood provides.
- Hood performance is presented as a property of the whole airflow arrangement. moduspol argues that missing replacement air can impair extraction and that opening a window can help [[./thread#^c45301420|moduspol]]. ajb emphasizes a short, straight, low-resistance exhaust path [[./thread#^c45307438|ajb]]. These comments support checking intake and ducting alongside the fan.
- Closable kitchens are proposed as a way to restrict the spread of cooking emissions and odors. infecto favors an exterior-facing kitchen with strong extraction [[./thread#^c45301012|infecto]], while kccqzy proposes sliding glass doors that can remain open for lower-temperature cooking [[./thread#^c45305016|kccqzy]]. buran77 particularly values protecting clothes and other living spaces from persistent smells [[./thread#^c45301994|buran77]].
- Filtration needs to match the pollutant being detected. jerlam says their Winix units sense VOCs and that HEPA filtration does little for those gases [[./thread#^c45304786|jerlam]]. adolph replies that activated-carbon filters can absorb VOCs, citing an external article [[./thread#^c45306398|adolph]]. This exchange identifies a distinction between filter types, but supplies no measured household VOC-removal performance.
- Monitoring can reveal emissions that occupants underestimate or fail to smell. kccqzy reports detecting PM2.5 before noticing smoke or burning [[./thread#^c45305079|kccqzy]]. jnordt values the AirQ Pro / Science's explanations of metrics, interfering factors and measurement errors over a simplified good/bad score [[./thread#^c45304651|jnordt]]. Neither report independently validates the devices' accuracy.

## Counterarguments

- Oil use does not produce uniformly large spikes in every account. Replying to infecto's broad claim, graeme says their tests showed large increases mainly when oil smoked or even tiny food fragments burned [[./thread#^c45301512|graeme]]. infecto accepts the importance of burning but maintains that frying also aerosolizes oil below its smoke point [[./thread#^c45303681|infecto]]. The exchange narrows the disagreement without resolving the relative contributions.
- Kitchen separation is not demonstrated to prevent whole-house particulate exposure. MostlyStable reports cooking-related sensor spikes across the house and behind several doors despite an outside-exhausting vent [[./thread#^c45304459|MostlyStable]]. groggler additionally observes that closing the kitchen still leaves the cook breathing its air [[./thread#^c45307554|groggler]]. Containment of other rooms and protection of the cook are different outcomes.
- Enclosure has a social cost for some households. AlecSchueler describes spending much of the day preparing family meals and wanting continued contact with family or guests [[./thread#^c45304563|AlecSchueler]]. JumpCrisscross argues that a good vent can preserve an open kitchen's social advantages [[./thread#^c45306780|JumpCrisscross]]. That comment's claim of equivalent health benefits is not supported by comparative measurements here.
- Avoiding oil or high heat can conflict with desired cooking results. xkcd-sucks argues that sufficiently hot pans prevent food boiling in its own moisture [[./thread#^c45314035|xkcd-sucks]]; chao- and nkrisc describe oil's contribution to crispness, flavor and even heating even in nonstick pans [[./thread#^c45301110|chao-]] [[./thread#^c45301283|nkrisc]]. These are culinary reasons for retaining emission-producing techniques, not evidence that their exposure is harmless.
- Cooking odors are not universally experienced as a comfort problem. piva00 is unbothered by the smell of a meal cooking [[./thread#^c45302240|piva00]], and bigstrat2003 enjoys Indian-food aromas [[./thread#^c45305068|bigstrat2003]]. lotsofpulp clarifies that their objection concerns persistent odors in walls, fabrics and fixtures after cooking [[./thread#^c45302448|lotsofpulp]]. Pleasant aromas, lingering contamination and irritating fumes should not be collapsed into one experience.
- A microwave-integrated hood is not proven inadequate merely by its form. mordechai9000 challenges that categorical judgment and asks whether exterior discharge and airflow are the relevant variables [[./thread#^c45304900|mordechai9000]]. lotsofpulp acknowledges assuming that microwave hoods move less air and bases the comparison on cursory searches [[./thread#^c45307266|lotsofpulp]].
- The discussion partly resolves a disagreement about ventilation versus filtration. Mistletoe challenges amo1111's description of ventilation as mainly dilution, pointing out that exhaust removes the air it displaces [[./thread#^c45301734|Mistletoe]]. amo1111 then agrees that outside ventilation is preferable and clarifies that the concern is finite clearance time during spikes, drawing on industrial spill experience rather than a measured domestic cooking failure [[./thread#^c45302445|amo1111]].
- Accepting frying emissions to avoid nonstick cookware is a contested personal-risk tradeoff. progbits prefers brief pollution plus ventilation and filters to feared PFAS ingestion [[./thread#^c45300891|progbits]]. onli disputes the assumed contamination from ordinary nonstick use [[./thread#^c45301029|onli]], and cassianoleal argues that PTFE is not a reason to discard existing pans provided it is not overheated [[./thread#^c45301043|cassianoleal]]. No comparative personal exposure or health measurements settle this choice.

## Assumptions

- This source unit is story 45268293, part 0, and contains no article body. The apparent original result—PM2.5 peaking at 11 µg/m³ and declining after frying stops—is available through jstanley's quotation [[./thread#^c45303399|jstanley]]. casualphysics says the broader purpose is finding a particle source for testing DIY filters [[./thread#^c45317154|casualphysics]]. The supplied discussion therefore does not expose a complete experimental protocol or dataset.
- A nonstick-looking pan did not establish oil-free cooking. Tade0 initially inferred that no oil was used [[./thread#^c45300702|Tade0]], but casualphysics directly corrected that inference [[./thread#^c45317184|casualphysics]] and separately specified an induction hob [[./thread#^c45317170|casualphysics]]. Arguments explaining the result solely through the supposed absence of oil rest on a premise the author rejected.
- Sensor location affects what a cooking or extraction test can establish. koolba proposes measuring across the room rather than immediately beside the pan to assess the extractor's net effect [[./thread#^c45304441|koolba]]. amluto proposes logging PM2.5 with the fan running but no cooking to investigate incoming pollution [[./thread#^c45310896|amluto]]. Neither proposed follow-up has a reported result in this part.
- Smell and deposited grease are imperfect substitutes for a measured fine-particle exposure. infecto treats detectable cooking smell as evidence of reduced air quality [[./thread#^c45301036|infecto]], while graeme distinguishes oil spattering from smoking and expresses uncertainty about atomization [[./thread#^c45305855|graeme]]. The discussion does not establish what fraction of visible grease or any particular odor corresponds to PM2.5.
- Automatic fan speed and indicator colors cannot be assumed to represent the same pollutant across appliances. Aaargh20318's purifier monitors several particle sizes and TVOC [[./thread#^c45301358|Aaargh20318]], whereas jerlam says their Winix sensors respond to VOCs [[./thread#^c45304786|jerlam]]. An appliance reacting to cooking establishes a sensor response, not a pollutant-specific concentration or verified removal rate.
- Nominal airflow and informal suction tests are proxies rather than installed pollutant-capture measurements. lotsofpulp supplies searched ratings of 300–400 CFM for microwaves versus 600–2,000 CFM for dedicated hoods [[./thread#^c45307266|lotsofpulp]]; ajb suggests a paper-holding test [[./thread#^c45307438|ajb]]. PeterStuer's claim that several minutes, sometimes ten, at maximum power establish an effective draft is also an experience-based assertion without a concentration trace [[./thread#^c45305833|PeterStuer]].
- Claims about Western or American housing should not be treated as representative prevalence estimates. infecto cites figures of roughly 10\% without hoods and 26\% of gas versus 36\% of electric households without outside venting [[./thread#^c45302342|infecto]]. dfxm12 identifies the linked study as Canadian and challenges its extension to the broader West [[./thread#^c45304044|dfxm12]]. The study itself is not supplied, and the competing personal housing histories do not resolve that geographic limitation.

## Conditions

- Expensive housing does not guarantee useful cooking exhaust in the listings lotsofpulp describes: they report million-dollar-plus homes with unvented island cooktops and estimate at least \$20,000 to remodel for ventilation [[./thread#^c45301585|lotsofpulp]]. Their observations and cost estimate concern their own housing search, not a general market survey or contractor quotation.
- Retrofitting outside exhaust can be constrained by building geometry. jerf reports that a direct opening behind their recirculating hood would encounter another adjoining exterior wall, making a fix difficult and expensive [[./thread#^c45301849|jerf]]. buran77 also describes kitchens far from outside walls and buildings where occupants cannot freely add vents or alter airflow [[./thread#^c45301994|buran77]].
- Requirements vary geographically and historically within the discussion. wffurr says outside-vented hoods are now required in their Massachusetts town [[./thread#^c45301749|wffurr]]. anikom15 assumes a hood is mandatory nationally [[./thread#^c45304839|anikom15]], while infecto replies that requirements are local and may permit recirculation [[./thread#^c45307429|infecto]]. The supplied comments do not establish a uniform US requirement.
- Layout preferences reflect particular housing experiences. infecto describes visiting Chinese condos with sliding glass doors and exterior windows or balcony doors that could be opened while cooking [[./thread#^c45302482|infecto]]. seec describes French open kitchens where noisy equipment also disturbs the living room [[./thread#^c45312381|seec]]. These observations illuminate practical differences without establishing national norms.
- The need for deliberate replacement air may depend on construction. amanaplanacanal suspects most homes leak enough already [[./thread#^c45305147|amanaplanacanal]]; AngryData distinguishes older homes from newer, more tightly sealed construction, using roughly 35 years as a dividing point [[./thread#^c45310323|AngryData]]. No blower-door or installed-airflow measurements substantiate that age rule here.
- Monitoring and filtration have purchase and replacement costs. Terretta mentions a \$750 German monitor selected through sensor reviews [[./thread#^c45288129|Terretta]], while kccqzy describes a much cheaper Temtop [[./thread#^c45305079|kccqzy]]. For the AEG model identified by Aaargh20318 [[./thread#^c45302118|Aaargh20318]], octo888 objects to a £100 filter price [[./thread#^c45307339|octo888]]. These reported prices do not establish comparative accuracy, filter life or total operating cost.

## Lived experiences

- crazygringo reports PM2.5 increasing from a baseline around 2 µg/m³ to approximately 200–400 µg/m³ when searing steak in cast iron [[./thread#^c45301367|crazygringo]]. This is a firsthand numerical observation, but the comment does not specify sensor model, ventilation, duration or repeatability.
- With a monitor about four meters from the stove, yoshuaw reports cooking usually staying below 5, burned vegetable oil reaching about 70, and overheated pancetta pushing both PM2.5 and PM10 above 999 [[./thread#^c45305575|yoshuaw]]. The commenter labels these values 'ppm'; that unit is unclarified in the supplied replies, so the figures should not be silently converted or compared directly with other commenters' µg/m³ readings.
- Tade0 reports their purifier displaying more than 100 µg/m³ whenever they cook with oil [[./thread#^c45318469|Tade0]], while also saying their usual cooking is largely stewed or steamed [[./thread#^c45311483|Tade0]]. Separately, kccqzy reports a Temtop PM2.5 display remaining at its maximum of 500 after excessive heating; its units are unstated, and a pegged display cannot identify the actual peak [[./thread#^c45305079|kccqzy]].
- AdmiralAsshat reports that frying an egg in oil in a wok repeatedly triggers a Coway purifier [[./thread#^c45300998|AdmiralAsshat]]. They estimate about five minutes before its indication normalizes after an egg, versus 15–20 minutes in the red after bacon [[./thread#^c45301288|AdmiralAsshat]]. These are appliance-indicator durations, not demonstrated clearance to a health-based threshold.
- Aaargh20318 reports a normally quiet bedroom purifier upstairs accelerating to full speed within roughly a minute of cooking downstairs [[./thread#^c45301358|Aaargh20318]]. This suggests rapid transport to another occupied room, but the multi-sensor appliance response does not identify which monitored pollutant triggered it.
- MostlyStable reports a particulate sensor detecting large cooking spikes across the house and behind several doors, despite describing their exterior-exhausting kitchen vent as good [[./thread#^c45304459|MostlyStable]]. The report supplies neither numerical concentrations nor a comparison with the doors open, so it challenges complete isolation without quantifying how much the doors helped.
- asdff reports greasy cabinet tops and oil accumulating inside the hood despite successful smoke-draw testing [[./thread#^c45304652|asdff]]. Their setup includes closing the kitchen door, opening windows and primarily using back burners directly under the hood [[./thread#^c45306382|asdff]]. The observed deposits show incomplete containment of cooking material, but do not measure its respirable fraction.
- whatevertrevor describes spicy Indian cooking causing eye watering and coughing, sometimes severely affecting the cook when ventilation is inadequate [[./thread#^c45306700|whatevertrevor]]. They also report oil and spice smells spreading to the kitchen's level and the floor above despite an open mesh door for replacement air [[./thread#^c45306622|whatevertrevor]]. nucleardog similarly describes burning eyes and coughing varying with hood effectiveness [[./thread#^c45306237|nucleardog]]. These are firsthand acute irritation accounts without pollutant-specific measurements.
- gaoshan moved the messiest Chinese-style cooking from an unvented island cooktop to an existing rolling island and induction hotplate in the garage, reporting a cleaner house at little expense [[./thread#^c45305136|gaoshan]]. seemaze moved much high-temperature cooking outdoors primarily to reduce indoor heat load and permit frying, smoking and searing [[./thread#^c45305063|seemaze]]. Neither reports the cook's resulting inhalation exposure.
- tensorlibb says opening a window makes the greatest difference in their experience and reports recurring calibration problems with a Temtop before choosing a Breathe Airmonitor Plus [[./thread#^c45301542|tensorlibb]]. Their reference to an NDIR sensor resembling an Aranet is a purchasing rationale; no before-and-after concentrations or reference calibration are supplied.
- In a Manhattan apartment built in 2018, tensorlibb reports a gas range with recirculating kitchen exhaust and a bathroom exhaust drawing toward the roof; operating the stove longer than about 15 minutes reportedly triggered their carbon-monoxide/fire alarm [[./thread#^c45301966|tensorlibb]]. The comment does not identify which alarm channel activated or provide a measured CO concentration.
- hombre\_fatal reports that making the bed, shaking sheets and vacuuming do not change their Winix indicator, whereas flatulence turns it red for minutes [[./thread#^c45304472|hombre\_fatal]]. In reply, jerlam says their Winix units measure VOCs [[./thread#^c45304786|jerlam]]. That ancestry offers a possible explanation for the different responses without demonstrating that bedroom dust was absent.
- ksab reports having performed laboratory cooking studies using a mass spectrometer and says frying oil emits more than readers might expect, recommending bacon as a test [[./thread#^c45301651|ksab]]. This is firsthand laboratory experience, but the comment provides no identified compounds, concentrations or health findings.
- Terretta says a \$750 device produced observations contrary to the article's apparent result [[./thread#^c45288129|Terretta]]. jstanley asks whether the disagreement concerns the peak, its direction or its decline after cooking [[./thread#^c45303399|jstanley]]. No substantive clarification appears in the supplied non-deleted comments, so this remains an unspecified contrary observation.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The relative roles of oil quantity, aerosolization, browning, smoking and actual burning remain unresolved. graeme emphasizes burning [[./thread#^c45301512|graeme]], infecto retains a role for aerosolization below the smoke point [[./thread#^c45303681|infecto]], and mttch attributes PM2.5 generation to the Maillard reaction while estimating gentle egg cooking around 150°C [[./thread#^c45301293|mttch]]. No supplied experiment measures those mechanisms separately or verifies that temperature.
- Commenters disagree about gas combustion's particulate contribution. ur-whale says gas itself generates considerable PM2.5 [[./thread#^c45301459|ur-whale]], crazygringo says it generates no significant particles [[./thread#^c45301367|crazygringo]], and mikeyouse says usually little PM2.5 but other harmful combustion products [[./thread#^c45301571|mikeyouse]]. This part provides no direct gas-versus-induction comparison and no measured NO2 or CO2 concentrations to resolve the broader exposure question.
- Particle composition and comparative toxicity are unanswered. cameldrv questions whether brake-metal dust, tire material and vegetable oil have different lung effects [[./thread#^c45304963|cameldrv]]. tomxor speculates that vaporized vegetable oil may be less toxic than engine emissions, while acknowledging possible changes after smoking [[./thread#^c45305880|tomxor]]. These are questions and intuitions, not evidence that cooking PM2.5 is benign or equivalent in toxicity to traffic particles.
- Low PM2.5 readings may not settle every emissions question, but the proposed limitations are not validated here. bhy claims cooking produces ultrafine particles that PM2.5 sensors miss, without identifying an instrument or comparison measurement [[./thread#^c45308576|bhy]]. tensorlibb reports calibration trouble [[./thread#^c45301542|tensorlibb]], while jnordt praises measurement documentation [[./thread#^c45304651|jnordt]]. No reference-instrument comparison establishes the accuracy or detection limits of the devices discussed.
- The relative benefit of enclosure versus stronger extraction remains contested. buran77 calls a closed kitchen essential for containing smells [[./thread#^c45301994|buran77]], JumpCrisscross claims a good vent can provide the same health benefits in an open kitchen [[./thread#^c45306780|JumpCrisscross]], and MostlyStable observes particles beyond several doors [[./thread#^c45304459|MostlyStable]]. These accounts measure or describe different outcomes and do not establish equivalent exposure reductions.
- Opening windows is reported to help, but its outdoor-air tradeoff is not measured in this part. tensorlibb reports improvement [[./thread#^c45301542|tensorlibb]]; amluto warns that exhaust operating with closed windows and no replacement-air provision can pull potentially dirty air through gaps [[./thread#^c45310896|amluto]]. infecto mentions California fires as a reason for becoming interested in air quality [[./thread#^c45307404|infecto]], but supplies no smoke measurements. There is no paired indoor/outdoor evidence or firsthand account here showing when opening a window worsened traffic or wildfire exposure.
- Long-term health consequences are not established by the reported peaks or acute irritation. JaggerFoo asks about professional chefs' health and says they have not heard of alarming studies [[./thread#^c45307377|JaggerFoo]]; that is not evidence that such studies do not exist or that occupational exposure is safe. ksab's laboratory recollection supplies no health outcomes either [[./thread#^c45301651|ksab]]. The coughing and eye irritation reported by whatevertrevor are real reported symptoms, but are not attributed through measurement to PM2.5, NO2 or another substance [[./thread#^c45306700|whatevertrevor]].
- This part offers no firsthand cognitive testing or demonstrated sleep changes associated with air quality. Aaargh20318 describes a purifier as normally inaudible at night, which concerns appliance noise rather than an observed sleep benefit [[./thread#^c45301358|Aaargh20318]]. crazygringo mentions CO2 as a combustion product without measuring it or reporting associated symptoms [[./thread#^c45301367|crazygringo]]. Claims about cognition, sleep or CO2-related effects therefore remain outside what this source unit can establish.
