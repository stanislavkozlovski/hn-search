# [IKEA made a smart air quality sensor to track indoor pollution](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34817391)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants discuss IKEA’s smart indoor air-quality sensor, its price, display, connectivity, integration with purifiers and home-automation systems, and the limits of its measurements. They compare the new device with IKEA’s earlier sensor and alternatives, debate accuracy and whether readings are useful for trends, and repeatedly note the absence of CO2 measurement. A substantial thread explains why CO2 readings can reveal poor ventilation even when people do not feel stuffy, weighing window-opening strategies, heating costs, humidity, and energy-recovery ventilation. Other topics include DIY sensors, air filtration, household pollution sources, and IKEA’s broader smart-home products.
Tags: IKEA, air quality sensor, indoor air quality, CO2, ventilation, home automation, air filtration

## Relevance

The discussion centers on an indoor air-quality sensor and covers PM2.5 readings, sensor accuracy and consistency, misleading indicator thresholds, and the absence of CO2 measurement. Firsthand accounts describe CO2 buildup in offices and bedrooms, perceived effects on concentration, stuffiness, and ventilation, along with cases where opening windows reduced CO2 but increased particle or other pollutant readings. Participants discuss practical responses including ventilation, filtration, and ERVs, as well as the tradeoffs of cold weather, heating, humidity, and outdoor pollution. The comments preserve disagreement about how noticeable CO2 is and whether monitoring is necessary, and note that sensor calibration and the distinction between real CO2 sensors and estimated readings matter. ## Arguments

- CO2 monitoring can make ventilation needs visible and teach occupants what their rooms require. netrus [[./thread#^c34817599|netrus]] advocates sensors in classrooms and offices; loudmax [[./thread#^c34818253|loudmax]] argues that recognizing stuffiness or a headache does not automatically teach someone to ventilate. Their case is for actionable feedback, rather than a demonstrated health benefit from owning a monitor.
- A monitor without CO2 sensing can still serve a useful kitchen-specific purpose. kixiQu [[./thread#^c34821880|kixiQu]] argues that cooking can produce substantial PM2.5 and VOCs without the cook noticing, while crazygringo [[./thread#^c34818447|crazygringo]] describes cooking as the main occasion when their otherwise low-pollution apartment generates large quantities of PM2.5.
- Repeatable detection of changes can be useful despite imperfect absolute accuracy. zihotki [[./thread#^c34817819|zihotki]] argues that trends and approximate readings often suffice; cptskippy [[./thread#^c34830377|cptskippy]] reports consistent responses across several inexpensive IKEA monitors, including responses to litter-box cleaning and cooking. This supports using them to identify events, while leaving their ability to establish absolute exposure unresolved.
- Numerical displays provide information that broad color categories conceal. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34821396|ahaucnx]] criticizes the reported green range of 0–35 μg/m³ and welcomes the new model's numerical display. Their argument is that occupants should be able to interpret the concentration themselves instead of treating a green light as sufficient reassurance.
- Filtration offers a practical response when outdoor particles or indoor allergens are the problem. blago [[./thread#^c34818766|blago]] broadly recommends air purifiers, and jabroni\_salad [[./thread#^c34819644|jabroni\_salad]] reports that filters help with pollen sensitivity even when operated by a simple timer. The firsthand account supports usefulness in that person's circumstances, without establishing effectiveness against every pollutant.
- Dedicated ventilation can address CO2 while limiting some disadvantages of open windows. dgacmu [[./thread#^c34818459|dgacmu]] recommends outdoor-air mixing or HRV/ERV installation where feasible; icee [[./thread#^c34820788|icee]] reports substantially lower occupied-house CO2 after installing an ERV. thadk [[./thread#^c34830784|thadk]] also prefers a window-mounted ERV to leaving a window open, although they supply no measurements.
- Monitoring can identify specific indoor activities worth changing before concluding that exposure is entirely imposed by the neighborhood. karmelapple [[./thread#^c34818977|karmelapple]] discovered rapid CO2 increases from a gas oven, while alias\_neo [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] identified stove lighting and refueling as particulate-producing events. Neither account supplies a controlled comparison of subsequent source-control interventions.

## Counterarguments

- ThePhysicist [[./thread#^c34818018|ThePhysicist]] argues that occupancy, room volume, ventilation, and perceived stuffiness make CO2 buildup predictable enough for scheduled airing without continuous monitoring. Their own small-office meter predictably turns red with the door closed. This is directly disputed by flemhans [[./thread#^c34818142|flemhans]] and not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819025|not\_kurt\_godel]], who report substantial CO2 differences they cannot reliably feel.
- The importance of domestic CO2 relative to particles and VOCs is contested. turtlebits [[./thread#^c34820268|turtlebits]] asserts that household CO2 is not a health risk and questions the added sensor cost; idiotsecant [[./thread#^c34819552|idiotsecant]] argues that people detect harmful concentrations themselves. valenterry [[./thread#^c34819601|valenterry]] counters that concentration can deteriorate before levels qualify as harmful. These are competing claims without supporting outcome measurements in the supplied exchange.
- Opening windows is not an unconditional air-quality improvement. pasc1878 [[./thread#^c34819430|pasc1878]] reports falling bedroom CO2 alongside rising VOC readings, and alias\_neo [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] reports much higher particulate readings outdoors and after opening a window near a busy road. These accounts challenge advice based exclusively on indoor CO2.
- Purification capabilities must be separated by pollutant. dfadsadsf [[./thread#^c34819063|dfadsadsf]] challenges the suggestion that HEPA filtration removes VOC gases and points to carbon filtration. In reply, not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819100|not\_kurt\_godel]] clarifies that their unit contains both HEPA and carbon filters; their original account explicitly says the purifier does not change CO2 [[./thread#^c34819025|not\_kurt\_godel]]. The clarification matters when interpreting their claimed removal of other pollutants.
- Houseplants are challenged as a meaningful remedy for occupied-room CO2. Ajedi32 [[./thread#^c34818513|Ajedi32]] cites an external estimate requiring hundreds of plants, and dgacmu [[./thread#^c34818459|dgacmu]] instead recommends ventilation. The ancestry also changes the interpretation of the original suggestion: happymellon [[./thread#^c34822693|happymellon]] subsequently says the houseplant recommendation was a joke.
- Knowing that air is poor does not guarantee an affordable or comfortable remedy. slavoingilizov [[./thread#^c34818744|slavoingilizov]] describes outdoor pollution as the local problem and questions what monitoring enables short of moving. boppo1 [[./thread#^c34818259|boppo1]] similarly questions whether responding would require freezing-weather window opening or expensive changes to heating and ventilation.
- Additional sensors and automation may provide little benefit when the appliance already responds automatically. d1sxeyes [[./thread#^c34835841|d1sxeyes]] reports difficulty finding useful additional air-quality or humidity automations, and Toutouxc [[./thread#^c34824323|Toutouxc]] notes that IKEA's Starkvind purifiers already contain PM2.5 sensors. This challenges the incremental value of a separate smart monitor, rather than the usefulness of filtration itself.
- Criticism of the new IKEA monitor's accuracy cannot simply inherit evidence about the older model. tecleandor [[./thread#^c34817855|tecleandor]], MaxPengwing [[./thread#^c34817816|MaxPengwing]], and danuker [[./thread#^c34817836|danuker]] identify that product mismatch. wartijn\_ [[./thread#^c34822404|wartijn\_]] additionally challenges calling the old model notoriously imprecise when the repeatedly cited review comes from a competing sensor vendor; that conflict warrants scrutiny without itself disproving the review.

## Assumptions

- This analysis concerns only story 34817391, part 0. Accounts such as mcjiggerlog's office monitoring [[./thread#^c34821653|mcjiggerlog]] and alias\_neo's household experiments [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] are self-reported observations from particular homes. They do not establish how common these conditions are among HN readers or the wider population; linked material is available here only through commenters' descriptions.
- Personal CO2 targets and health-based limits are not interchangeable. crazygringo [[./thread#^c34818447|crazygringo]] aims for roughly 550 ppm, while flemhans [[./thread#^c34818142|flemhans]] describes 1500 ppm as above recommended limits without identifying the recommendation. Similarly, ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34821396|ahaucnx]] compares an instantaneous-looking PM indicator range with a reported WHO annual guideline below 5 μg/m³; the different averaging periods limit what that comparison establishes about a particular reading.
- Automatic CO2 calibration depends on assumptions about exposure to a background concentration. wkat4242 [[./thread#^c34827750|wkat4242]] questions whether an occupied home ever reaches the assumed 400 ppm, especially in a city. thebruce87m [[./thread#^c34824273|thebruce87m]] suggests an unoccupied, well-ventilated room should return to outdoor levels. Neither statement establishes the actual background or calibration behavior in another user's home.
- An air-quality reading should not automatically be interpreted as a direct CO2 measurement. wkat4242 [[./thread#^c34819564|wkat4242]] distinguishes actual CO2 sensing from VOC-derived eCO2 and strongly criticizes the latter. lostmsu [[./thread#^c34819984|lostmsu]] and Toutouxc [[./thread#^c34826695|Toutouxc]] explicitly correct assumptions that the IKEA device measures CO2 at all.
- Shared components and vendor expectations are weaker evidence than a comparative test. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34829056|ahaucnx]] expects AirGradient and Aranet CO2 performance to be similar because both use Senseair modules, and expects Awair accuracy to resemble AirGradient's [[./thread#^c34821665|ahaucnx]]. chewmieser [[./thread#^c34818037|chewmieser]] recalls a favorable comparison of Atmotube with expensive equipment. The supplied comments contain none of those comparative datasets.
- Treating one reading as whole-house exposure assumes adequate mixing and representative placement. yalogin [[./thread#^c34821085|yalogin]] explicitly questions this extrapolation. titaniumdafodil [[./thread#^c34819790|titaniumdafodil]] reports large floor-to-floor CO2 differences affected by fan operation, so their apparent benefit from circulation depends on household mixing and unreported outdoor-air exchange; it does not establish a general CO2-removal capability for recirculation.

## Conditions

- Reported February 2023 prices made basic particle monitoring substantially cheaper than many multipollutant options. progbits [[./thread#^c34817575|progbits]] describes an approximately \$10 old IKEA monitor plus a \$3 ESP module, while input\_sh [[./thread#^c34817669|input\_sh]] lists that monitor at \$16. smhg [[./thread#^c34818376|smhg]] reports the new model at 999 CZK, about €42; tpmx [[./thread#^c34819752|tpmx]] says that includes 21\% VAT. pjc50 [[./thread#^c34819232|pjc50]] reports no UK availability at that time.
- The cost of adding genuine CO2 measurement is disputed, and component prices are not finished-device prices. progbits [[./thread#^c34817918|progbits]] quotes \$25 in bulk for an SCD40; MichaelZuo [[./thread#^c34818095|MichaelZuo]] extrapolates an eventual retail price of at least \$200. billiallards [[./thread#^c34820778|billiallards]] instead points to an assembled board around \$60. Those products and cost assumptions are not equivalent, while mcjiggerlog [[./thread#^c34821653|mcjiggerlog]] reports actually purchasing a CO2 monitor for about €90.
- Budget and willingness to assemble hardware change the available options. KerrickStaley [[./thread#^c34818667|KerrickStaley]] reports buying a \$67 AirGradient kit; ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34822498|ahaucnx]] estimates \$200–300 for the commercially targeted AirGradient ONE. KaiserPro [[./thread#^c34818251|KaiserPro]] bought used Awair units for about £50 versus £200 new. adolph [[./thread#^c34820161|adolph]] highlights recurring replacement-filter expenditure in addition to the initial sensor and purifier purchases.
- The value of outdoor ventilation depends on local outdoor pollution. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34821396|ahaucnx]] particularly prioritizes CO2 outside heavily polluted areas, while slavoingilizov [[./thread#^c34818744|slavoingilizov]] describes a locality where the main pollution source is outdoors. alias\_neo's busy-road measurements [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] illustrate a household where opening windows can sacrifice low indoor particulate levels.
- Winter recommendations have substantial climatic limits. arrrg [[./thread#^c34818500|arrrg]] describes brief airing, approximately 20°C indoor temperatures, and extra clothing as workable because furnishings and walls retain heat. throwaway14356 [[./thread#^c34818342|throwaway14356]] proposes much broader acclimation to open windows and little heating, but kgermino [[./thread#^c34819808|kgermino]] objects even at roughly −1°C outdoors, and Germanika [[./thread#^c34825788|Germanika]] describes freezing and burst-pipe risks in much colder regions.
- Humidity and cooling or heating requirements can reverse the comfort tradeoff. crazygringo [[./thread#^c34820624|crazygringo]] reports extremely dry winter air and continued humidifier use; Semaphor [[./thread#^c34819098|Semaphor]] reports indoor humidity above 60\% and uses a dehumidifier. icee [[./thread#^c34820788|icee]] describes hot, humid outdoor air as a barrier to ordinary ventilation. thombat's condensation concern [[./thread#^c34819613|thombat]] is therefore contextual, rather than evidence that every cracked window causes mold.
- Building design and retrofit access constrain ventilation improvements. hellweaver666 [[./thread#^c34833835|hellweaver666]] describes renovating an old Dutch house with a heat pump and triple glazing without frame vents, then adding an ERV because the envelope had become sealed; routing ducts required extensive improvisation. dgacmu [[./thread#^c34818459|dgacmu]] explicitly notes that HVAC modifications and HRV/ERV installation can be expensive or impossible in some situations.

## Lived experiences

- mcjiggerlog [[./thread#^c34821653|mcjiggerlog]] reports that a roughly €90 CO2 monitor revealed how quickly a small home office could reach around 2000 ppm and prompted more ventilation. Their assertion that this concentration noticeably affects mental faculties is not accompanied by a measured cognitive change or a quantified time-to-threshold.
- flemhans [[./thread#^c34818142|flemhans]] cannot feel a difference between 500 and 1500 ppm and finds keeping rooms below 1000 ppm difficult and frustrating. not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819025|not\_kurt\_godel]] similarly reports little detectable sensation up to 2500 ppm, later clarifying their HEPA-plus-carbon setup [[./thread#^c34819100|not\_kurt\_godel]]. In another reply, not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819150|not\_kurt\_godel]] reports exceeding 800 ppm within an hour from background without constant ventilation.
- dghughes [[./thread#^c34818536|dghughes]] reports a NetAtmo reading of 1000 ppm during a Christmas gathering in an old house without an air handler. The room felt stuffy, and opening a window made it feel much better. Cooling and other changes occurred alongside CO2 reduction, so the account does not isolate the cause of the improvement.
- crazygringo [[./thread#^c34818447|crazygringo]] adjusts a living-room window several times daily to maintain roughly 550 ppm. They describe a gap of approximately 1 mm to 1 cm even below freezing, a cold corner near the window, and little apparent increase in heating expenditure [[./thread#^c34818493|crazygringo]]. The energy-cost observation is informal rather than a controlled bill comparison.
- pasc1878 [[./thread#^c34819430|pasc1878]] [[./thread#^c34819425|pasc1878]] reports that bedroom CO2 rises during sleep, falls quickly after opening the window, and is accompanied by increased pollutant readings, identified as VOCs in the more specific comment. These near-duplicate posts describe one account; neither gives concentrations, identifies the entering source, or reports a change in sleep quality.
- titaniumdafodil [[./thread#^c34819790|titaniumdafodil]] reports that a five-minute airing followed by continuous central fan operation kept CO2 below 700 ppm throughout their home for about six hours. Without that circulation pattern, the second floor reached the 1000s while the first stayed near 500 ppm. Occupancy, building leakage, and outdoor-air supply are not specified.
- icee [[./thread#^c34820788|icee]] reports installing a Panasonic ERV with considerable effort and reducing occupied-house CO2 from consistently above 1200 ppm to approximately 500 ppm. They describe heat and humidity as the reasons ordinary outdoor-air intake was difficult. No corresponding health, cognition, sleep, or energy-consumption measurements are supplied.
- alias\_neo [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] built a PM2.5/PM10 monitor and used it for several weeks after installing a log stove. They report average indoor readings around 1–2 μg/m³ and readings around 20–30 μg/m³ outdoors or after opening a window, near a fairly busy road. The comment does not identify which particle fraction each numerical reading represents or verify the road's contribution independently.
- Using the same monitor, alias\_neo [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] reports no detectable living-room PM increase when the log stove burns properly, but approximately 70 μg/m³ during refueling or poor lighting and up to 200 μg/m³ when substantial smoke escapes. Readings can take hours to return to normal. They had not yet added CO2 monitoring, so low particulate readings do not establish low concentrations of combustion gases.
- karmelapple [[./thread#^c34818977|karmelapple]] reports CO2 jumping from about 600 ppm to above 1200 ppm almost immediately whenever their gas oven was turned on. Separately, crazygringo [[./thread#^c34818447|crazygringo]] describes massive PM2.5 generation while cooking without numerical values. These are different households and pollutant observations; neither account measures cooking-related NO2.
- cptskippy [[./thread#^c34830377|cptskippy]] bought several older IKEA monitors, added ESP modules, and logged their outputs in Home Assistant. They report sufficiently consistent responses to identify litter-box cleaning and observe air-quality changes in rooms immediately above the kitchen stove in a three-story house. This demonstrates perceived event sensitivity and spatial propagation, without a reference-instrument accuracy comparison.
- dalyons [[./thread#^c34820177|dalyons]] reports a PurpleAir indoor monitor showing large PM2.5 increases during humidifier use. robg [[./thread#^c34819669|robg]] interprets an LG purifier/sensor response as suggesting aerosolized heavy metals. somehnguy [[./thread#^c34820610|somehnguy]] reports purifier warnings after showers in two homes and suspects humidity. These observations establish device responses, while the explanations remain different and unverified.
- jabroni\_salad [[./thread#^c34819644|jabroni\_salad]] reports that air filters have helped with pollen sensitivity and operates them on a timer without additional sensors. The account contains no particle measurements, symptom scores, or comparison with another intervention.
- KerrickStaley [[./thread#^c34818667|KerrickStaley]] reports enjoying assembly and use of an AirGradient kit measuring PM2.5, CO2, temperature, and humidity, with WiFi logging, but describes CO2 calibration loss and an understood need for outdoor exposure about every two weeks. chewmieser [[./thread#^c34818037|chewmieser]] reports roughly a week of Atmotube battery life and Bluetooth-only synchronization, illustrating practical monitoring constraints distinct from measurement accuracy.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The size and cause of health or cognitive effects remain unsettled in this source. mcjiggerlog [[./thread#^c34821653|mcjiggerlog]] asserts noticeable mental impairment around 2000 ppm; turtlebits [[./thread#^c34820268|turtlebits]] denies a household CO2 health risk; valenterry [[./thread#^c34819601|valenterry]] distinguishes concentration effects from harmful exposure. No described controlled study or cognitive test adjudicates these claims. pasc1878's overnight readings [[./thread#^c34819430|pasc1878]] likewise establish no sleep-quality effect.
- Brief full-window airing versus a continuous crack remains a household-specific dispute with different objectives. shafyy [[./thread#^c34819062|shafyy]] favors brief drafts and later clarifies that the claim concerns heating efficiency [[./thread#^c34824123|shafyy]]. crazygringo [[./thread#^c34819114|crazygringo]] reports unacceptable CO2 buildup within half an hour and finds repeated full airing too cold. No comparative energy measurements resolve the thermal-efficiency question, and the participants do not share the same building or target.
- CO2 calibration requirements are disputed across sensors and operating modes. peterbraden [[./thread#^c34819896|peterbraden]] describes SCD40-series calibration difficulties; woefblaf123 [[./thread#^c34819977|woefblaf123]] extends periodic outdoor exposure to NDIR sensors generally. fotta [[./thread#^c34820082|fotta]] counters that they disable automatic calibration on an SCD30, perform one outdoor baseline calibration, and rely on a claimed lifetime drift specification of ±50 ppm. This does not establish a universal procedure or accuracy guarantee for every model.
- Carbon-filter service life remains unresolved. Filligree [[./thread#^c34821535|Filligree]] repeats advice that VOC absorption stops after about a week regardless of printer use, while acknowledging uncertainty about verification. not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819834|not\_kurt\_godel]] replaces filters every six to twelve months according to manufacturer guidance and the appliance indicator, and also wants independent effectiveness measurements. Neither supplies a measured breakthrough curve or pollutant-specific removal test.
- Humidifier and shower responses do not establish what the sensor detected chemically. robg's heavy-metal explanation [[./thread#^c34819669|robg]], dalyons's humidifier-associated PM2.5 spikes [[./thread#^c34820177|dalyons]], and somehnguy's humidity explanation [[./thread#^c34820610|somehnguy]] remain unseparated possibilities involving emitted material and sensor response to moisture. No supplied analysis identifies metals, quantifies their concentrations, or establishes the associated health exposure.
- The new IKEA model's absolute accuracy is not established. ahaucnx [[./thread#^c34821396|ahaucnx]] explicitly says a test cycle is needed; bborud [[./thread#^c34821760|bborud]] only tentatively identifies its particle sensor. jahnu's accuracy question about inexpensive Chinese CO2 monitors [[./thread#^c34823209|jahnu]] also receives no answer. noja [[./thread#^c34821748|noja]] raises an additional unanswered usability question about whether the new IKEA model retains an annoyingly intermittent fan.
- The coverage of a single sensor remains uncertain. yalogin [[./thread#^c34821085|yalogin]] asks whether one location can represent a room or house; cptskippy [[./thread#^c34830377|cptskippy]] and titaniumdafodil [[./thread#^c34819790|titaniumdafodil]] provide examples of inter-room transmission and floor-specific differences, respectively. These examples establish reasons to care about placement, without supplying a validated sampling strategy or whole-house exposure estimate.
- Furniture-related formaldehyde exposure is contested without measurements. blago [[./thread#^c34818824|blago]] attributes much household formaldehyde pollution to IKEA furniture. CharlesW [[./thread#^c34819973|CharlesW]] responds that IKEA stopped adding formaldehyde in 1993 and cites company wording; blago [[./thread#^c34828560|blago]] disputes the dating and whether that wording covers glue and particleboard. The exchange supplies neither emission measurements nor a demonstrated household exposure level.
- NO2 and wildfire smoke have little or no direct coverage in this unit. not\_kurt\_godel [[./thread#^c34819025|not\_kurt\_godel]] includes NO2 among pollutants their purifier supposedly controls but supplies no concentration or removal measurement. londons\_explore [[./thread#^c34818207|londons\_explore]] proposes hypothetical NOx and particle-composition sensing, which Doxin [[./thread#^c34867278|Doxin]] questions on signal-strength grounds. No supplied firsthand account concerns wildfire smoke; alias\_neo's measured smoke episodes [[./thread#^c34819961|alias\_neo]] specifically involve an indoor log stove.
