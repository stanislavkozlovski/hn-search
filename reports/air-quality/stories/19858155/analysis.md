# [Air Pollution in Our Homes](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=19858155)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants discuss indoor pollution from cooking, cleaning products, furnishings, building materials, and ventilation, while weighing concerns about exposure against uncertainty about long-term health effects and the risk of sensational reporting. Practical advice focuses on externally vented range hoods, opening windows, air exchange systems, air cleaners, and reducing indoor sources. A substantial exchange questions whether houseplants meaningfully clean indoor air and compares them with ventilation and filtration. Commenters also debate consumer air-quality monitors, especially their accuracy for particulates and formaldehyde, and share experiences with cooking fumes, household sensitivities, and the limits of interpreting risk measurements.
Tags: indoor air quality, ventilation, cooking emissions, air filtration, VOCs, air quality sensors, health risk

## Relevance

The discussion includes firsthand indoor PM readings during cooking, wildfire smoke, nearby traffic, and other household activities, as well as reported symptoms and filtration choices. It also covers CO2, ventilation and window-opening tradeoffs, range-hood effectiveness, and uncertainty in consumer VOC and formaldehyde sensors. ## Arguments

- Household monitoring can reveal exposure patterns that comfort or building appearance misses. graeme [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] reports readings that changed decisions about cooking and opening windows; Rudebwoy10 [[./thread#^c19861530|Rudebwoy10]] found unexpectedly high PM2.5 in a new condo during California fires. These accounts support investigating particular activities and locations, without establishing the accuracy of every reading.
- Capturing cooking emissions at their source is a recurring recommendation. jartelt [[./thread#^c19859524|jartelt]] advocates using the exhaust hood, while jly [[./thread#^c19860482|jly]] specifically argues for outdoor exhaust because cooking produces fine particles and gas appliances also produce NO2 and CO. The NO2 statement is a pollutant-source claim, not a firsthand NO2 measurement.
- Airtight construction and adequate ventilation need to be considered together. swampthinker [[./thread#^c19862051|swampthinker]] describes heat-recovery air exchange accompanying tighter European buildings, and explicitly answers lxmorj’s concern about rapid CO2 buildup by pointing to air-exchange requirements [[./thread#^c19930466|swampthinker]]. The argument is for deliberate ventilation rather than relying on uncontrolled leakage.
- Ventilation and source removal are proposed for indoor chemical emissions. graeme [[./thread#^c19861872|graeme]] recommends identifying and removing sources where possible, otherwise venting; nextos [[./thread#^c19861550|nextos]] favors lower-emitting construction materials and an activated-carbon filter stage. These are proposed remedies, without comparative removal measurements in their comments.
- Prioritization matters because indoor pollutants are not interchangeable. jly [[./thread#^c19860676|jly]] summarizes a linked disability-adjusted-life-year analysis as ranking PM2.5 first and mold second, arguing for attention to particles and humidity. This is a commenter’s account of an external analysis, not a risk calculation reproduced in the supplied thread.
- Reducing scented consumer products is presented as another practical intervention. tomohawk [[./thread#^c19863300|tomohawk]] recommends avoiding scented detergents, dryer sheets, and fabric softeners, suggesting vinegar as an alternative; forgotmypw3 [[./thread#^c19861660|forgotmypw3]] also emphasizes cleaners and detergents. Neither supplies before-and-after exposure measurements.
- Discomfort and illness matter even when changes in life expectancy are unknown. code\_duck [[./thread#^c19862983|code\_duck]] argues that respiratory symptoms, headaches, sensitivities, and interference with smelling or tasting deserve consideration alongside mortality. Their discussion explicitly distinguishes personal sensitivities from effects on everyone.

## Counterarguments

- The houseplant recommendation receives a substantive scaling objection. pdog [[./thread#^c19861096|pdog]] invokes NASA research on benzene and formaldehyde removal, but martey [[./thread#^c19861248|martey]] quotes a reanalysis estimating roughly 1,000 plants in a 10-by-10-foot office to match one air change per hour, or about one plant per square foot using the best performers. hoorayimhelping [[./thread#^c19862663|hoorayimhelping]] emphasizes sealed experimental conditions, and soulofmischief [[./thread#^c19864787|soulofmischief]] disputes treating a few plants as replacements for filtration or air exchange.
- Turning on a hood does not establish effective extraction. bluGill [[./thread#^c19859654|bluGill]] objects that recirculating hoods return much of the pollution indoors; jly [[./thread#^c19860482|jly]] distinguishes these from externally exhausted hoods. graeme [[./thread#^c19864176|graeme]] reports persistently high particle readings while frying with a hood running, although the comment does not establish that hood’s configuration or capture efficiency.
- Opening windows can increase exposure when outdoor sources are nearby. graeme [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] reports an open window admitting a neighbor’s barbecue emissions and also reports elevated readings around an idling construction truck. Rudebwoy10’s wildfire-period indoor measurement [[./thread#^c19861530|Rudebwoy10]] further illustrates why outdoor conditions matter, although that account does not identify open windows as the entry route.
- A higher particle reading does not, by itself, establish greater overall harm across different smoke sources. nitrogen [[./thread#^c19860222|nitrogen]] finds cooking emissions less irritating than wildfire emissions, while lukeschlather [[./thread#^c19861496|lukeschlather]] describes severe smoke-related coughing on a day official reports did not classify as bad. These accounts complicate the cooking-versus-wildfire comparison without demonstrating that either source is generally safer.
- rayiner [[./thread#^c19859445|rayiner]] [[./thread#^c19861208|rayiner]] questions whether familiar cooking practices could impose large unnoticed harms and asks for meaningful outcomes such as years of life lost. jartelt [[./thread#^c19859524|jartelt]] counters with longstanding indoor wood-stove use despite respiratory harm; mbostleman [[./thread#^c19859953|mbostleman]] argues that delayed, nonspecific effects could remain unnoticed. The historical-familiarity argument and its rebuttals do not quantify present-day household risk.
- hcurtiss [[./thread#^c19864670|hcurtiss]] argues that hazard benchmarks can provoke disproportionate concern relative to everyday risks. However, the Thanksgiving cancer-risk example is explicitly invented, and coolgeek [[./thread#^c19869799|coolgeek]] corrects the driving-rate conversion to 107 per million, which hcurtiss [[./thread#^c19870369|hcurtiss]] accepts. The comparison also juxtaposes lifetime excess cancer risk with a yearly death rate, so it does not establish the relative risk of an actual cooking exposure.
- The alarming formaldehyde account is substantially qualified by its follow-ups. jly [[./thread#^c19868451|jly]] raises a possible mg/m³-versus-ppm issue, explaining that 0.1 mg/m³ is about 0.08 ppm at 25°C. salex89 [[./thread#^c19888112|salex89]] replies that the device also displays ppm, but concludes after further investigation that its absolute readings should not be trusted. The ancestry supports neither a simple units-only explanation nor acceptance of the original readings as confirmed exposure.
- The discussion explicitly corrects an unsupported appliance generalization. After vallode [[./thread#^c19859146|vallode]] groups microwaves with toasters as things to avoid, NickBusey [[./thread#^c19859969|NickBusey]] points out that the article discussion provides no microwave evidence. vallode [[./thread#^c19866734|vallode]] acknowledges the inference was erroneous; toaster observations should therefore not be extended to microwaves.

## Assumptions

- Sensor recommendations often depend on secondhand validation or brand reputation. graeme [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] cites reviews comparing the LaserEgg with laboratory sensors; Rudebwoy10 [[./thread#^c19862846|Rudebwoy10]] recalls reading that Awair sensors are certified; clairity [[./thread#^c19862506|clairity]] trusts Airthings more for radon than VOCs or CO2. None presents their own reference-instrument comparison, and clairity’s explanation of the other sensors’ likely quality is explicitly speculative.
- clairity [[./thread#^c19862506|clairity]] assumes imperfect CO2 readings are sufficient to trigger ventilation around 800–1000 ppm. That is a personal operating heuristic, not a demonstrated threshold for cognitive or sleep impairment, and its practical usefulness depends on whether opening a window improves the overall exposure situation.
- Interpreting a concentration requires attention to units and exposure duration. jly [[./thread#^c19862578|jly]] quotes different formaldehyde recommendations—0.016 ppm for a workday, 0.05 ppm from CARB, and 0.08 ppm over 30 minutes from WHO. These are attributed guideline claims with differing contexts, not interchangeable definitions of a universally safe instantaneous reading.
- The lower-emission-material argument partly assumes that natural materials, certain finishes, or reduced odor indicate reduced harmful emissions. nextos [[./thread#^c19861550|nextos]] [[./thread#^c19876799|nextos]] describes oiled floors and soap-treated furniture as low-emitting, whereas londons\_explore [[./thread#^c19875589|londons\_explore]] broadly objects that wood treatments emit VOCs. jspash [[./thread#^c19861843|jspash]] hopes measurements will validate a natural mattress preference but acknowledges that the mattress might also emit fumes.
- Treating smell itself as harmful pollution is an additional assumption. closeparen [[./thread#^c19864554|closeparen]] asks whether everything smellable counts as pollution, while soulofmischief [[./thread#^c19864814|soulofmischief]] asks what mechanism makes scented laundry products harmful and what 'pollution' means. Odor, irritation, detectable emissions, and quantified health risk are not established as equivalent by these comments.
- The fiber discussion relies on competing biological assumptions. frutiger [[./thread#^c19860551|frutiger]] invokes mucus clearance, dsfyu404ed [[./thread#^c19860717|dsfyu404ed]] invokes evolutionary exposure to particles, and jacobush [[./thread#^c19863117|jacobush]] explicitly guesses that acrylic is sufficiently inert. rtkwe [[./thread#^c19862029|rtkwe]] instead emphasizes persistence and tissue damage. These comments do not establish the safety of the textile or insulation exposures that prompted the discussion.

## Conditions

- Outdoor conditions create geographic limits on window-opening advice. downtide [[./thread#^c19860181|downtide]] keeps a cooking window open year-round in southern UK conditions described as rarely below freezing; macNchz [[./thread#^c19860766|macNchz]] notes that some American climates discourage this. kaybe [[./thread#^c19867456|kaybe]] stresses that draftiness can be welcome in one season and undesirable in another.
- Housing observations conflict by region and dwelling type. Against bluGill’s claim that most household hoods recirculate [[./thread#^c19859654|bluGill]], rootusrootus [[./thread#^c19860523|rootusrootus]] reports that single-family homes in their US Pacific Northwest experience usually vent outdoors, with recirculation more common in small retrofitted apartments. Neither account establishes national prevalence.
- The ventilation context includes building requirements and renovation opportunities. verelo [[./thread#^c19865019|verelo]] says a Toronto duplex renovation required ERVs, describing them as inexpensive; swampthinker [[./thread#^c19862051|swampthinker]] associates modern European efficiency and leakage standards with heat-recovery ventilation. These are location-specific accounts, without installed prices or evidence that the same requirements apply elsewhere.
- Effective cooking extraction can involve substantial purchase and retrofit costs. fma [[./thread#^c19861756|fma]] calls the Fotile hood expensive; sushisource [[./thread#^c19863479|sushisource]] identifies cabinet reconstruction as a barrier. fma [[./thread#^c19864998|fma]] managed the replacement with minor custom woodwork and a carpenter father’s help, a resource that cannot be assumed for other households.
- Exhaust capacity also interacts with building pressure and replacement air. ip26 [[./thread#^c19862250|ip26]] identifies stack effect, range hoods, bathroom fans, dryers, and combustion appliances as possible depressurization sources. velosol [[./thread#^c19863982|velosol]] discusses makeup air, California testing requirements, and roughly 300 CFM as a point to consider mechanical replacement air; that number is a commenter’s rule of thumb, not an established universal requirement.
- Furniture affordability is disputed. lotsofpulp [[./thread#^c19863380|lotsofpulp]] says higher-quality natural furniture is unaffordable for many; nextos [[./thread#^c19863575|nextos]] counters with an approximately \$50 raw-pine IKEA table. kaybe [[./thread#^c19867409|kaybe]] suggests used or locally free furniture where transport is feasible. These are prices and availability claims from this 2019 discussion, not current guarantees.
- Monitoring has cost and usability constraints. jly [[./thread#^c19862609|jly]] reports approximately \$60 for one formaldehyde badge test; Rudebwoy10 [[./thread#^c19862846|Rudebwoy10]] declines an additional \$75 for radon capability they do not need. Forbo [[./thread#^c19861053|Forbo]] wants devices without internet dependence, and graeme [[./thread#^c19861059|graeme]] says their CO2 monitors are unconnected and recalls that the LaserEgg can operate offline.

## Lived experiences

- Firsthand particle-monitor observations: graeme [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] reports readings reaching what they call an unsafe range during frying, especially browning, high-setting toasting, a neighbor’s barbecue with the window open, and an idling construction truck. They provide no concentrations, durations, or reference calibration, but describe using the portable monitor to change household decisions.
- Firsthand cooking-versus-outdoor comparison: surfmike [[./thread#^c19860049|surfmike]] says indoor cooking readings exceeded outdoor readings during California wildfires and consequently planned an externally vented hood. Separately, jly [[./thread#^c19860544|jly]] reports unhealthy PM2.5 readings upstairs within minutes of cooking and measurable upstairs CO increases while using a gas oven. jly attributes the distribution to HVAC circulation; no numerical readings are supplied.
- Firsthand numerical PM2.5 report: Rudebwoy10 [[./thread#^c19861530|Rudebwoy10]] says an Awair measured indoor PM2.5 reaching 150 µg/m³ in a brand-new California condo during fires. They had expected better filtration and raised the issue with the building. The account supplies a concentration but no exposure duration, paired outdoor measurement, or independent accuracy check.
- Firsthand but subsequently questioned chemical readings: salex89 [[./thread#^c19861689|salex89]] reports difficulty finding indoor locations below a purported 0.1 ppm formaldehyde threshold, lower readings outdoors or with a wide-open window, and anxiety about concentrations around 0.3 ppm. Their later conclusion that the device cannot be trusted for absolute levels [[./thread#^c19888112|salex89]] is essential to interpreting the experience.
- Firsthand alternative testing: jly [[./thread#^c19862631|jly]], who owns a boxed mattress and laminate flooring, reports reassuring results from a one-time formaldehyde badge test and describes its accuracy as 0.002 ppm. They later identify the F-50 badge [[./thread#^c19868456|jly]]. No actual home concentration is disclosed, so the account cannot establish that similar products are universally low-emitting.
- Firsthand gas-monitor observations: clairity [[./thread#^c19862073|clairity]] reports Wave Plus VOC spikes from several activities, including closing windows at night, and explicitly says the device does not directly measure particles. lxmorj [[./thread#^c19906996|lxmorj]] reports rapid CO2 buildup after getting a Netatmo sensor in a well-sealed setting. Neither supplies concentrations, occupancy details, or measured health effects.
- Firsthand cooking-associated symptoms: not\_a\_moth [[./thread#^c19860376|not\_a\_moth]] reports headaches around lunchtime at two poorly ventilated San Francisco cafes, but not at other cafes. At home, oven use by a roommate who avoided the fan and cold outdoor air coincided with headaches, whereas another roommate’s ventilated cooking did not. The commenter considered a nocebo explanation; no pollutant was measured or isolated.
- Firsthand wildfire symptoms: lukeschlather [[./thread#^c19861496|lukeschlather]] reports coughing so severely during a Seattle-area smoke event that they took a taxi for the final seven blocks while carrying groceries, despite reassuring official reports. Their explanation that particles were too large for the reporting system is their attribution, not a demonstrated size measurement. nitrogen [[./thread#^c19860222|nitrogen]] separately reports less irritation from cooking than wildfire emissions.
- Firsthand cleaner and fragrance sensitivity: code\_duck [[./thread#^c19862025|code\_duck]] describes sneezing, red eyes, feeling ill, and a headache after nearby Windex spraying, against a background of food allergies and celiac disease. Their concern about airborne toast allergens is not accompanied by allergen measurements. clairity [[./thread#^c19862111|clairity]] reports coughing and sneezing with Simple Green and switching to dish soap and baking soda.
- Firsthand hood satisfaction based on smell: fma [[./thread#^c19861756|fma]] says that after installing a Fotile hood, someone five feet from frying could no longer smell the cooking. This is a reported sensory improvement, without particle or combustion-gas measurements. It therefore cannot directly resolve graeme’s separate report of high frying readings with a hood operating [[./thread#^c19864176|graeme]].
- Firsthand comfort accounts: macNchz [[./thread#^c19860766|macNchz]] describes newer air-conditioned homes as stuffy compared with a drafty childhood home; e40 [[./thread#^c19861029|e40]] can feel drafts through old Bay Area sash windows and values them after reading about CO2. downtide [[./thread#^c19860181|downtide]] reports chest discomfort in unventilated rooms and routinely creates drafts. None supplies measurements connecting these sensations specifically to CO2.
- Firsthand ventilation practice: buildzr [[./thread#^c19861330|buildzr]] runs an HRV for 15 minutes each hour. verelo [[./thread#^c19865019|verelo]] describes a large perceived improvement after installing required ERVs during a Toronto renovation. These establish actual use and satisfaction, but not measured pollutant reductions or the adequacy of either operating schedule elsewhere.
- Firsthand plant benefits are principally experiential: hoorayimhelping [[./thread#^c19862663|hoorayimhelping]] reports a substantial psychological boost, kaybe [[./thread#^c19867421|kaybe]] perceives changes in humidity and smell, and clairity [[./thread#^c19861909|clairity]] enjoys easy-growing plants while explicitly remaining unsure of filtration effectiveness. These accounts support comfort or aesthetic benefits without demonstrating meaningful pollutant removal.
- Firsthand purifier use does not establish health or sleep outcomes. mensetmanusman [[./thread#^c19862381|mensetmanusman]] has used Filtrete purifiers as children’s white-noise generators for more than seven years and believes childhood dust contributed to their own asthma. skookumchuck [[./thread#^c19864755|skookumchuck]] combines a drafty house with HVAC filtration and describes convincing themselves that it improves their air. Neither reports a controlled exposure comparison or measured improvement in asthma or sleep.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The magnitude of individual health benefit remains unresolved. rayiner [[./thread#^c19861175|rayiner]] [[./thread#^c19861208|rayiner]] asks for concrete outcomes from plants or reducing cooking exposure, while nextos [[./thread#^c19862558|nextos]] invokes possible carcinogenicity, including formaldehyde, and precaution despite limited studies. The supplied comments do not connect a measured household exposure reduction to quantified changes in cancer, respiratory disease, or life expectancy.
- CO2-related cognition and sleep are not directly evaluated in this source unit. macNchz [[./thread#^c19860766|macNchz]] mentions recent CO2 studies without presenting results, lxmorj [[./thread#^c19906996|lxmorj]] reports buildup without concentrations, and mensetmanusman [[./thread#^c19862381|mensetmanusman]] describes purifier white noise without sleep outcomes. The thread cannot establish cognitive impairment thresholds or that ventilation or filtration improved sleep.
- NO2 exposure remains particularly undermeasured here. jly [[./thread#^c19860482|jly]] names it as a gas-range combustion concern, but their firsthand gas-oven measurement concerns CO [[./thread#^c19860544|jly]]. Likewise, graeme’s truck-related observation [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] does not provide NO2 data. These accounts leave actual indoor and traffic-related NO2 concentrations and intervention effects unresolved.
- Consumer-device comparisons are unsettled. clairity [[./thread#^c19863446|clairity]] links monitor comparisons, but Rudebwoy10 [[./thread#^c19863853|Rudebwoy10]] questions their age, exclusion of newer models, and inconsistent overall grades. nsporillo [[./thread#^c19860950|nsporillo]] asks whether PurpleAir’s PM1 capability offers more than a similarly priced LaserEgg, without receiving a firsthand comparison. The comments do not establish a validated best device across particle sizes and gases.
- Affordable identification of particular hazardous VOCs remains an open question. sizzle [[./thread#^c19864746|sizzle]] asks for a reasonably priced carcinogenic-VOC detector; jly [[./thread#^c19862609|jly]] does not know a sufficiently accurate consumer formaldehyde sensor and instead reports badge testing. londons\_explore [[./thread#^c19875644|londons\_explore]] speculates about a \$1 mass-spectrometer design but provides no working device or validation.
- The best ventilation–filtration balance depends on circumstances that are not jointly measured here. graeme [[./thread#^c19860589|graeme]] observes both indoor cooking emissions and outdoor sources; not\_a\_moth [[./thread#^c19860376|not\_a\_moth]] describes fan-noise and cold-air objections; verelo [[./thread#^c19865019|verelo]] favors mechanical exchange. No supplied account compares these options using simultaneous indoor/outdoor PM2.5, CO2, NO2, comfort, and cost measurements.
- Broad rankings of smoke sources remain unsupported within the export. telotortium [[./thread#^c19862443|telotortium]] infers that reductions in historical smoke exposure make other VOCs a leading concern; whttheuuu [[./thread#^c19862839|whttheuuu]] dismisses much secondhand-smoke concern relative to vehicle emissions; segfaultbuserr [[./thread#^c19860266|segfaultbuserr]] quotes a claim that fireplace smoke is at least as harmful as cigarette smoke. Without comparable doses and durations, these competing claims do not settle personal exposure priorities.
- Material and fiber hazards also remain unresolved. londons\_explore [[./thread#^c19875616|londons\_explore]] asserts that used furniture has mostly finished off-gassing, but supplies no measurements. jjtheblunt [[./thread#^c19860421|jjtheblunt]] labels fiberglass carcinogenic, while twistiti [[./thread#^c19864195|twistiti]] invokes fiber-size differences and rtkwe [[./thread#^c19862029|rtkwe]] emphasizes clearance and damage. The discussion supplies neither verification of those classifications nor exposure-specific evidence sufficient to quantify the risks.
