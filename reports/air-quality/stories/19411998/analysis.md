# [Associations of Cognitive Function with Carbon Dioxide and Ventilation (2016)](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=19411998)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants debate research linking elevated indoor CO2 and poor ventilation with reduced cognitive performance. They question the evidence and its relevance to submarines, flights, meetings, and bedrooms; distinguish CO2 exposure from oxygen depletion and other accumulated pollutants; and discuss HVAC monitoring, building codes, ventilation practices, temperature, and the limits of anecdotal experience.
Tags: CO2, cognitive performance, ventilation, indoor air quality, HVAC, research evidence

## Relevance

Focuses on CO2, ventilation, and cognitive performance, with competing interpretations of studies and submarine experience. Comments include reports of stuffy rooms, headaches, sleep concerns, office sensor practices, and disagreement about whether CO2 itself or other poorly ventilated pollutants explain effects. ## Arguments

- lamontcg [[./thread#^c19412607|lamontcg]] reports study findings in which focused activity changed little, but initiative and strategy declined somewhat at 1,000 ppm CO2 and sharply at 2,500 ppm. In reply to military studies reporting no cognitive impairment, lamontcg [[./thread#^c19412657|lamontcg]] quotes another experiment finding impaired response selection during one hour at 4\% CO2. The argument is that cognitive effects may depend on which abilities a test measures; these are reports of studies, not commenters' personal measurements.
- z3t4 [[./thread#^c19414312|z3t4]] argues that CO2 may principally indicate inadequate air exchange, with other gases, particles, and fumes causing impairment. This supports investigating ventilation and multiple pollutants even if CO2's independent effect remains disputed. keithpeter [[./thread#^c19412922|keithpeter]] similarly relays a speaker's priorities of mould, particulate matter, VOCs, then CO2; that hierarchy is the speaker's judgment rather than a comparison established in this thread.
- Short individual meetings do not necessarily mean short room exposure. Replying to ianlevesque's workplace limit on long meetings [[./thread#^c19412076|ianlevesque]], staticassertion [[./thread#^c19412135|staticassertion]] points out that a room can host successive meetings. crankylinuxuser [[./thread#^c19412222|crankylinuxuser]] describes shared rooms used back to back and asks whether contaminants accumulate through the day. This is a plausible concern raised from occupancy patterns, without measured accumulation or clearance rates.
- Opening doors or windows is presented as an accessible way to improve exchange: ehnto [[./thread#^c19413016|ehnto]] describes using both in offices with limited ventilation. However, 1e-9 [[./thread#^c19415431|1e-9]] warns that outside air entering through leaks can bring pollen and other pollutants, and proposes supplying filtered outside air under positive pressure. That proposal combines ventilation with filtration; it supplies no evidence that a recirculating purifier alone controls CO2 or that the proposed installation achieved particular pollutant reductions.
- dangravell [[./thread#^c19415938|dangravell]] advocates an airtight building with whole-house ventilation, ideally including heat recovery. 0db532a0 [[./thread#^c19413370|0db532a0]] also favors heat exchangers where outdoor temperatures make ventilation costly in lost heat. Their practical argument is for deliberate air exchange rather than dependence on weather-driven leakage or occupants tolerating cold drafts; neither supplies installation costs or measured results.
- dbg31415 [[./thread#^c19412828|dbg31415]] quotes a report of a proofreading experiment in which participants at 77°F missed roughly half the errors, compared with roughly a quarter at 67°F. This introduces temperature as another possible influence on office performance, separate from CO2. It is a quoted study claim, not a controlled experiment conducted by the commenter.

## Counterarguments

- ggreer [[./thread#^c19412362|ggreer]] challenges claims of impairment from small CO2 increases by citing reported submarine concentrations of 300–11,300 ppm and an older experiment finding no deficits on multiple cognitive tests after two weeks at 40,000 ppm in physically fit young airmen. The same comment reports poor sleep, headaches, and hand tremor before cognitive problems. Its evidence therefore challenges particular cognitive findings without demonstrating an absence of other health effects or establishing safety for ordinary occupants.
- chroem- [[./thread#^c19412101|chroem-]] initially calls the issue effectively solved in renovated commercial buildings because CO2 monitoring is integrated into HVAC, and describes individual room or zone controls [[./thread#^c19412276|chroem-]]. However, after JamesCoyne [[./thread#^c19412149|JamesCoyne]] reports different practice in small commercial buildings, chroem- [[./thread#^c19412165|chroem-]] acknowledges that high-rise versus smaller projects may explain their differing experience. The ancestry narrows the original universal claim.
- antisthenes [[./thread#^c19412211|antisthenes]] and caymanjim [[./thread#^c19412679|caymanjim]] distinguish elevated CO2 from insufficient oxygen; lvturner [[./thread#^c19412622|lvturner]] accepts that correction to their original description of stuffy rooms. On adding oxygen, quickthrower2 [[./thread#^c19412129|quickthrower2]] argues it would help only with oxygen deficiency, while \_uhtu [[./thread#^c19412148|\_uhtu]] invokes Apollo 13 to argue that oxygen does not eliminate a CO2 problem. These replies challenge conflating two exposures, but do not themselves establish effects on cognition at office concentrations.
- Several replies dispute attributing flight exhaustion to CO2. userbinator [[./thread#^c19412273|userbinator]] cites aircraft ventilation rates; travisjungroth [[./thread#^c19412391|travisjungroth]] proposes reduced oxygen availability at cabin altitude; sl1ck731 [[./thread#^c19412548|sl1ck731]] proposes continuous small stabilizing movements. WalterGR [[./thread#^c19412227|WalterGR]] notes that the study abstract says nothing about exhaustion. These are alternative explanations, with no in-flight CO2 measurements supplied to adjudicate them.
- Against 0db532a0's claim that opening trickle vents negates double glazing's benefit [[./thread#^c19413370|0db532a0]], fulafel [[./thread#^c19413545|fulafel]] distinguishes reduced conductive heat loss through the glass from heat loss accepted to ventilate the room. Thus, the thread disputes whether ventilation cancels the insulation benefit, while still recognizing ventilation's thermal cost.
- The cooking discussion challenges treating all electric cooking alike. dangravell [[./thread#^c19419727|dangravell]] points out that induction is electric; 0db532a0 [[./thread#^c19429609|0db532a0]] acknowledges its higher power but objects to cookware compatibility, cycling, and coarse power settings. DanBC [[./thread#^c19470570|DanBC]] directly disputes the claim that warped cookware cannot work with induction. These are contested usability claims, not evidence comparing indoor emissions.
- saagarjha [[./thread#^c19412200|saagarjha]] rejects the suggestion by 0815test [[./thread#^c19412112|0815test]] that additional meeting-room CO2 appreciably heats the room through a greenhouse effect. This correction separates a room's thermal comfort problems from that proposed mechanism.

## Assumptions

- ggreer's physiological argument [[./thread#^c19412362|ggreer]] assumes a simplified mixing model: lung air initially at 5\% CO2, with 80\% replaced per breath. The calculated starting concentrations of 10,320 versus 10,800 ppm are a thought experiment, not measured physiology or a cognitive dose-response experiment. Likewise, transferring results from fit young airmen to other populations and tasks requires assumptions that the thread does not test.
- The formaldehyde and consumer-sensor claims are secondhand. mattjaynes [[./thread#^c19412457|mattjaynes]] recounts a talk in which DHH's wife reportedly showed signs of formaldehyde poisoning after moving into a newly built house, prompting industrial testing because consumer devices were allegedly inaccurate. The supplied comment contains neither a confirmed diagnosis nor concentrations, device comparisons, or calibration results.
- Two brands are useful corroboration but do not establish accuracy: awakeasleep [[./thread#^c19412355|awakeasleep]] reports approximately 850 ppm without identifying calibration procedures or sensor models. Likewise, tjohns's observation that breathing near a wall sensor activates HVAC [[./thread#^c19412174|tjohns]] shows a local control response, not that the sensor accurately represents the room or that adequate fresh air reaches occupants.
- Interpreting discomfort or fatigue as CO2 impairment assumes a cause that these anecdotes do not measure. lvturner [[./thread#^c19412175|lvturner]] reports rooms lacking fresh-feeling air, while tootie [[./thread#^c19412235|tootie]] reports fatigue after successive eight-hour meeting days. Neither supplies gas concentrations, cognitive tests, or a comparison separating air quality from meeting duration, temperature, or other factors.
- The apparent five-minute onset claim from PorterDuff [[./thread#^c19412096|PorterDuff]] is explicitly identified as a joke by BooneJS [[./thread#^c19412300|BooneJS]], after oh\_sigh [[./thread#^c19412289|oh\_sigh]] asks about occupancy and room size. leemailll's suggestion that effects begin before two hours [[./thread#^c19412116|leemailll]] is a suspicion. Neither should be treated as a measured exposure threshold.
- Proven [[./thread#^c19412229|Proven]] dismisses concern about a closed bedroom by comparing the oxygen needs of four talking people for two hours with one sleeping person for eight hours. That comparison supplies no room volume, ventilation rate, CO2 concentration, or sleep outcome, and assumes oxygen demand settles the question of overnight exposure.

## Conditions

- Building type and jurisdiction substantially limit the HVAC claims. chroem- [[./thread#^c19412136|chroem-]] grounds their account in US professional work; JamesCoyne [[./thread#^c19412149|JamesCoyne]] describes small commercial practice and says codes typically prescribe fresh-air rates by space and occupancy rather than requiring demand-controlled ventilation. swampthinker's assertion that only CO detectors are mandatory [[./thread#^c19412146|swampthinker]] provides no jurisdiction. No supplied code text establishes a universal monitoring requirement.
- UK housing examples involve competing thermal and acoustic needs. keithpeter [[./thread#^c19413663|keithpeter]] describes small trickle vents, approximately 6 mm by 30 cm, and older houses with air bricks and substantial leakage. 0db532a0 [[./thread#^c19419680|0db532a0]] says the vents undermine sound isolation even when closed. Those accounts make outdoor temperature, street noise, and building construction relevant to whether occupants will accept ventilation, without quantifying the effects.
- Housing tenure and installation access affect the available intervention. 1e-9 [[./thread#^c19415431|1e-9]] proposes a ducted filtered-air supply, with a window kit as an alternative for renters or people unwilling to penetrate a wall. The same comment warns that natural leakage depends on wind and temperature differences and can admit outdoor pollutants; suitability therefore depends on both the building and incoming air.
- Operating and equipment costs can work against ventilation. chewz [[./thread#^c19412814|chewz]] attributes reduced office air exchange to heating and cooling savings, and weak bus air conditioning to driver fuel-saving bonuses. JamesCoyne [[./thread#^c19412199|JamesCoyne]] says some clients see controls mainly as an equipment expense and resist automation. These are commenters' explanations of incentives, not audited energy or exposure comparisons.
- antisthenes [[./thread#^c19412206|antisthenes]] cites portable household CO2 sensors costing \$60–100 in this 2019 discussion. In response, mettamage [[./thread#^c19412294|mettamage]] describes a former client's integration of consented occupant data with environmental sensors, and antisthenes [[./thread#^c19412411|antisthenes]] sees value in combining several environmental measurements. The thread provides neither accuracy comparisons nor a cost-benefit calculation for portable monitoring versus building integration.
- Cooking changes face household performance preferences. In the UK housing discussion, keithpeter [[./thread#^c19413663|keithpeter]] identifies open gas cooking as a source of fumes. 0db532a0 [[./thread#^c19419665|0db532a0]] strongly prefers gas cooking and an on-demand gas boiler, and later describes difficulties with low-power electric cooking and cookware [[./thread#^c19429609|0db532a0]]. These are contextual obstacles to switching appliances, not measured evidence that any cooking preference outweighs an exposure benefit.

## Lived experiences

- awakeasleep [[./thread#^c19412355|awakeasleep]] reports testing a LEED Platinum workplace with two brands of CO2 monitor and seeing readings settle around 850 ppm once people arrive. This is the clearest numerical firsthand CO2 measurement in the supplied discussion. It documents one occupied building, without simultaneous performance testing or a ventilation intervention.
- tjohns [[./thread#^c19412174|tjohns]] reports that several strong breaths near conference-room CO2 sensors make the HVAC run at full blast. This is firsthand evidence of responsive controls at their office, with no numerical readings or measured change in room-wide air quality.
- ehnto [[./thread#^c19413016|ehnto]] describes three workplaces using domestic wall-mounted air conditioners rather than the sophisticated controls described by chroem-: a high-rise with ventilation mainly in a central area, a two-floor building where a stuffy conference room required an open door, and another building with opening windows. lvturner [[./thread#^c19412175|lvturner]] separately reports stale-feeling rooms in the UK and Hong Kong, while acknowledging uncertainty about installation or code compliance.
- i\_am\_proteus [[./thread#^c19412572|i\_am\_proteus]] recalls slightly increasing submarine oxygen levels to improve crew alertness, subject to an upper limit because of fire risk. They also report headaches when atmospheric controls failed and CO2 rose, while saying normal crew exhaustion made subtler effects hard to notice. The account explicitly disclaims a controlled experimental setting and supplies no concentrations.
- Thermal experiences conflict. dbg31415 [[./thread#^c19412195|dbg31415]] feels groggy around 75°F or above, prefers 72°F or lower, and likes roughly 65°F in winter. caymanjim [[./thread#^c19412691|caymanjim]] reports difficulty functioning above 70°F. abootstrapper [[./thread#^c19412231|abootstrapper]], replying directly to the claim that warmth lowers performance, instead prefers about 76°F. These preferences do not establish a single temperature that optimizes everyone's cognition.
- curtis [[./thread#^c19412093|curtis]] describes flying and long meetings as unusually exhausting, and says flying feels less tiring with an empty adjacent seat [[./thread#^c19415316|curtis]]. tootie [[./thread#^c19412235|tootie]] reports feeling depleted after two consecutive days of eight-hour meetings. Separately, caymanjim [[./thread#^c19412668|caymanjim]] reports no noticeable altitude effects around 8,000 feet but noticeable effects around 12,000 feet. All are subjective experiences without exposure measurements or cognitive testing.
- keithpeter [[./thread#^c19412922|keithpeter]] recognizes inadequate ventilation in new housing from firsthand work dealing with condensation problems. This supplies practical experience with moisture-related building problems, but no measured pollutant concentrations or demonstrated health outcome.
- iceninenines [[./thread#^c19412466|iceninenines]] reports returning a strongly smelling mattress that left the spare room smelling for weeks. They attribute it to excessive bromine-based fire retardants, but supply no chemical testing. The firsthand evidence is the odor and product return; the chemical identification and broader toxicity claims remain unverified.
- chewz [[./thread#^c19412814|chewz]] describes crowded buses as suffocating with weak air conditioning and prohibited window opening, while describing electric trams as excessively cold. They identify this discomfort as one reason for cycling. The city is unspecified, and the account contains no CO2, oxygen, particulate, or temperature readings.

## Unresolved disagreements

- Whether typical indoor CO2 directly impairs cognition remains contested between ggreer's military-study argument [[./thread#^c19412362|ggreer]], lamontcg's task-specific findings [[./thread#^c19412607|lamontcg]], and z3t4's ventilation-proxy explanation [[./thread#^c19414312|z3t4]]. jey [[./thread#^c19412070|jey]] asks whether the focal paper has been replicated; no answer establishes replication. danw1979 [[./thread#^c19412832|danw1979]] also asks whether they have misread the abstract as showing better scores in a well-ventilated condition with added CO2; the supplied discussion leaves that interpretation unanswered.
- The thread does not establish a reliable concentration-by-duration threshold for ordinary rooms. crankylinuxuser [[./thread#^c19412222|crankylinuxuser]] asks how quickly impairment occurs and whether consecutive meetings compound exposure. awakeasleep [[./thread#^c19412355|awakeasleep]] questions whether an approximately 1,000 ppm control target is sufficiently protective, but their approximately 850 ppm observation includes no measured impairment. Neither a safe boundary nor an onset time follows from these comments.
- Ordinary bedroom sleep effects remain unanswered. buttslasher69 [[./thread#^c19412017|buttslasher69]] asks about sleeping in a closed room; Simon\_says [[./thread#^c19412156|Simon\_says]] and jfkw [[./thread#^c19412315|jfkw]] point to an external discussion without supplying its observations. ggreer's reported sleep effects in the military-exposure discussion [[./thread#^c19412362|ggreer]] do not establish what happens in an ordinary bedroom. There are no supplied overnight readings or before-and-after sleep accounts.
- The practical benefit of each ventilation approach is unquantified. ehnto [[./thread#^c19413016|ehnto]] reports using open windows and doors, 1e-9 [[./thread#^c19415431|1e-9]] proposes filtered outdoor supply, and dangravell [[./thread#^c19415938|dangravell]] favors whole-house ventilation with heat recovery. No paired measurements compare those approaches on CO2, incoming pollutants, health, comfort, noise, or cost. Outdoor pollution could change whether opening a window is beneficial, but this unit provides no contemporaneous indoor/outdoor comparison.
- Temperature sensitivity and adaptation remain uncertain. gubbrora [[./thread#^c19415328|gubbrora]] asks whether acclimatization changes the reported temperature effects; ordu [[./thread#^c19413494|ordu]] speculates about cognition contributing to bodily heat production. Neither mechanism is tested in the supplied comments, and the conflicting preferences of abootstrapper [[./thread#^c19412231|abootstrapper]] and caymanjim [[./thread#^c19412691|caymanjim]] leave a universal office-temperature recommendation unsupported.
- This source unit does not resolve the approved question's PM2.5, NO2, traffic, or wildfire aspects. keithpeter [[./thread#^c19412922|keithpeter]] mentions particulate matter generally and gas-cooking fumes separately [[./thread#^c19413663|keithpeter]], while 1e-9 [[./thread#^c19415431|1e-9]] discusses pollen and outdoor pollutants. There are no supplied PM2.5 or NO2 concentrations, traffic or wildfire exposure accounts, measured cooking emissions, or quantified filtration outcomes. Those broader questions cannot be answered from this CO2-centered discussion.
