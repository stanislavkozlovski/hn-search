# [Smog in Western U.S. Starts Out as Pollution in Asia, Researchers Say](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=13795379)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Commenters debate how pollution originating in Asia contributes to western U.S. smog and what that means for domestic environmental regulation, trade, and consumer responsibility. Some argue that stricter rules can shift manufacturing and pollution abroad; others counter that U.S. air quality improved under regulation and that economic outcomes depend on more than environmental rules. The thread also discusses China and India’s renewable-energy efforts, pollution trends, political incentives, and the limits of personal or international interventions.
Tags: transboundary-pollution, EPA, environmental-regulation, international-trade, renewable-energy, politics

## Relevance

Although much of the thread concerns regulation and international pollution transport, it includes firsthand accounts of visible haze and burning lungs in Asian cities and discussion of how outdoor air quality varies by place and time. Most policy claims are not personal exposure evidence. ## Arguments

- ArtDev [[./thread#^c13795388|ArtDev]] argues that preventing air pollution is easier than restoring degraded air, using Shanghai and New Delhi as reasons to preserve pollution controls. This is a prevention argument, without quantified health effects or remediation evidence.
- tropo [[./thread#^c13806840|tropo]] argues that stricter U.S. regulation relocates industry to less regulated countries, whose pollution then crosses the ocean, and concludes that relaxing domestic controls would improve U.S. air quality. The comment supplies a causal hypothesis rather than measurements establishing that net effect.
- kogepathic [[./thread#^c13798179|kogepathic]] argues that conspicuous, personally experienced pollution creates pressure for faster cleanup. jackcn [[./thread#^c13800544|jackcn]] similarly attributes industrial and energy changes in China and India to demands for cleaner air. Neither supplies measured changes in personal exposure.

## Counterarguments

- Replying directly to tropo, dragonwriter [[./thread#^c13806910|dragonwriter]] and nikdaheratik [[./thread#^c13825250|nikdaheratik]] argue that U.S. pollution fell substantially after stronger regulation despite imported smog. nikdaheratik interprets the article as identifying limits to additional domestic improvements, rather than evidence that earlier regulation increased pollution. These historical and attribution claims are not independently demonstrated in the supplied text.
- Responding to tluyben2’s report of burning lungs in Hong Kong [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]], seanmcdirmid [[./thread#^c13800804|seanmcdirmid]] says Hong Kong was then at '91' and generally had relatively mild pollution by Chinese standards. This supplies a conflicting assessment of severity, but the unspecified reading and potentially different exposure dates do not establish that the visitor’s episode was mild.

## Assumptions

- tropo’s argument [[./thread#^c13806840|tropo]] assumes that regulation caused the relevant industrial relocation and that transported pollution outweighed the domestic pollution avoided. Neither link is quantified; the replies from dragonwriter [[./thread#^c13806910|dragonwriter]] and nikdaheratik [[./thread#^c13825250|nikdaheratik]] dispute the resulting conclusion.
- The travel accounts use visible haze and bodily sensations as indicators of air conditions: ArtDev [[./thread#^c13800040|ArtDev]] describes striking pollution while flying through Shanghai, and kogepathic [[./thread#^c13798179|kogepathic]] describes severely reduced visibility in Beijing. These observations do not identify pollutant composition or establish a calibrated relationship between visibility, inhaled dose, and health risk.

## Conditions

- Geographic comparisons are explicitly relative. seanmcdirmid [[./thread#^c13797343|seanmcdirmid]] says Shanghai is not considered particularly bad within China, while tluyben2 [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]] finds Beijing worse than Shanghai/Suzhou and describes a particularly uncomfortable Hong Kong visit. Relative city rankings cannot establish an absolute health threshold or conditions throughout each city.
- The transboundary framing limits purely local explanations and remedies. nikdaheratik [[./thread#^c13825250|nikdaheratik]] interprets the article as saying half of remaining pollution comes from outside the country, while ebbv [[./thread#^c13797561|ebbv]] emphasizes the difficulty of regulating foreign sources and doubts tariffs would have their intended effect. The supplied export contains no article text establishing the fraction’s pollutant, region, period, or methodology.
- The observations concern different times in this March 2017 discussion: ArtDev [[./thread#^c13800040|ArtDev]] had visited Shanghai the preceding week; kogepathic [[./thread#^c13798179|kogepathic]] describes a Beijing layover the previous year; seanmcdirmid [[./thread#^c13800804|seanmcdirmid]] gives a Hong Kong number 'ATM.' tluyben2’s concern about India [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]] comes partly from a linked ranking, while their personal visit was about ten years earlier and did not leave a specific memory of bad air. These are not simultaneous comparisons.

## Lived experiences

- tluyben2 [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]] reports frequent visits to Shanghai/Suzhou that generally felt acceptable, worse conditions in Beijing, and immediate 'burning lungs' after leaving a plane in Hong Kong. Google Now repeatedly advised staying indoors, which felt sensible to them. This is firsthand respiratory discomfort and receipt of an automated warning; they report no personal instrument readings or demonstrated improvement after sheltering.
- ArtDev [[./thread#^c13800040|ArtDev]], replying to seanmcdirmid’s characterization of Shanghai as comparatively less polluted, reports finding the city’s pollution shocking during a recent visit, especially while flying through it. The account provides visual experience without symptoms or quantified exposure.
- kogepathic [[./thread#^c13798179|kogepathic]], replying within a discussion of cleaner energy in China and India, recalls a Beijing layover when the runway could not be seen from the terminal and pollution was visible inside the building. This is firsthand evidence of perceived indoor as well as outdoor pollution, without measurements distinguishing indoor sources, outdoor infiltration, or the terminal’s ventilation performance.

## Unresolved disagreements

- Measurement quality cannot be assessed. seanmcdirmid’s Hong Kong value of '91' [[./thread#^c13800804|seanmcdirmid]] has no named index, pollutant, units, source, averaging interval, or instrument. It cannot reliably be interpreted as a PM2.5 concentration or used to evaluate sensor accuracy. The travel accounts provide no corresponding indoor/outdoor measurement series.
- Health attribution remains limited to reported experience. tluyben2’s burning-lung episode [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]] establishes acute discomfort in perceived polluted conditions, but does not identify the responsible pollutant or demonstrate lasting injury. This unit supplies no PM2.5-, NO2-, or CO2-specific exposure measurements, and no reported cognition or sleep outcomes.
- The practical value of staying indoors is untested here. tluyben2 [[./thread#^c13800631|tluyben2]] regarded the advice favorably, while kogepathic’s separate account [[./thread#^c13798179|kogepathic]] describes visible pollution inside an airport terminal. Together they leave indoor protection uncertain rather than establishing a successful intervention. No supplied account compares opening versus closing windows, filtration, ventilation changes, or exposures from cooking, traffic, or wildfire smoke.
- The pace and adequacy of improvement remain disputed. kaybe [[./thread#^c13800129|kaybe]], replying to kogepathic’s optimism, says pollution had declined for two or three years based on conversations with researchers, explicitly expressing uncertainty about publication. bigbugbag [[./thread#^c13799653|bigbugbag]] argues that improvement is insufficient and consequences would persist for decades. Neither provides a defined pollutant series or exposure endpoint; a declining trend and substantial remaining harm could coexist.
