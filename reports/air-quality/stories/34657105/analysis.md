# [Study links adoption of EV with less air pollution and improved health](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34657105)

[[./thread|Discussion]]

## Description

Participants discuss a study associating greater electric-vehicle adoption with lower local air pollution and fewer asthma-related emergency visits. The thread considers whether the analysis establishes causation and how local tailpipe emissions compare with pollution from power generation, manufacturing, battery materials, tires, and brakes. Participants debate EVs as a near-term improvement versus broader strategies that reduce car use, expand public transit, cycling, and walkability. Other exchanges address vehicle noise, heavy trucks and buses, affordability, charging infrastructure, mineral supply chains, recycling, and the environmental costs of consumption.
Tags: electric vehicles, air pollution, public health, transportation, vehicle lifecycle, urban mobility

## Relevance

Comments discuss local exposure to vehicle exhaust and particulate pollution, including firsthand experience around traffic and claims about asthma-related ER visits. The thread distinguishes tailpipe emissions from tire and brake particles and includes disagreement about how much EV adoption changes exposure. ## Arguments

- pengaru [[./thread#^c34657806|pengaru]] argues that removing combustion engines from streets reduces exhaust released immediately beside people breathing, even if electricity generation still burns fuel elsewhere. They also argue that stationary power plants offer better opportunities for exhaust treatment. This is an exposure-based argument, without a quantified comparison of pollution or health outcomes.
- hedora [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]], replying to a claim about tire dust, distinguishes combustion pollutants from particulate emissions: they argue that EVs eliminate tailpipe pollutant classes and reduce brake dust while increasing tire wear. Their comment supports evaluating different pollution sources separately; it supplies no PM2.5 measurements or particle-size breakdown.
- dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658257|dangrossman]] describes the study as comparing EV registrations with asthma-related emergency-room visit rates by ZIP code. blamazon [[./thread#^c34658268|blamazon]] emphasizes its localized emissions claim. The health evidence discussed is therefore a reported area-level association, with no firsthand clinical account or numerical study results supplied.
- StreamBright [[./thread#^c34658355|StreamBright]] points to European dining areas without cars, while blamazon [[./thread#^c34657837|blamazon]] favors walking, cycling and small electric vehicles for reducing nearby traffic disturbance. blamazon [[./thread#^c34658407|blamazon]] considers electrification a practical transition in car-dependent places and reducing the number of cars an additional improvement.
- nickysielicki [[./thread#^c34658013|nickysielicki]] argues that an urban diesel truck idling beside a coffee shop should have a diesel particulate filter. In response to concern about disproportionately polluting trucks and buses, Symbiote [[./thread#^c34659292|Symbiote]] describes emissions-based vehicle charges and bus-fleet changes. These comments propose targeting exposure sources, without reporting measured intervention outcomes.

## Counterarguments

- melling [[./thread#^c34657764|melling]] quotes another HN discussion claiming that modern vehicle pollution mostly comes from tire dust and that lightweight transport could outperform heavy EVs for street-level pollution. hedora [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]] replies that this reasoning concerns particulates and overlooks combustion pollutants. Neither comment establishes the relevant emissions fractions or their contribution to inhaled PM2.5.
- funstuff007 [[./thread#^c34658522|funstuff007]] questions whether electrifying passenger sedans would substantially help residents when a small number of diesel trucks and unusually dirty vehicles might dominate pollution. Their assertion that asthma rates near the cited building would remain unaffected until trucks and buses electrify is a hypothesis, unsupported by measurements in the supplied discussion.
- nickysielicki [[./thread#^c34657844|nickysielicki]] initially suggests pandemic-related reductions in urban activity could confound the study. dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658257|dangrossman]] replies that COVID effects presumably occurred across ZIP codes; MBCook [[./thread#^c34687582|MBCook]] subsequently states that the study covered 2013–2019. That reported timeline answers the specific COVID objection, while leaving other causal questions open.
- nickysielicki [[./thread#^c34658013|nickysielicki]] asserts that long-haul trucks on rural interstates are not causing cancer through their emissions, explicitly acknowledging a need for a citation. jjtheblunt [[./thread#^c34658284|jjtheblunt]] challenges the implied absence of exposed people by asking about truck drivers and surrounding traffic. No exposure or cancer-risk data resolves that exchange.
- amalgamated\_inc [[./thread#^c34657893|amalgamated\_inc]] disputes broad expectations of quieter cities from EV adoption, arguing that tire and wind noise often dominate. Their interpretation of a linked video [[./thread#^c34659560|amalgamated\_inc]] adds that heavier vehicles and wider tires can increase noise and that measured Teslas were as loud as combustion cars. These are secondhand reports of acoustic measurements, with no numerical readings supplied.

## Assumptions

- A causal interpretation of the ZIP-code comparison described by dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658257|dangrossman]] requires accounting for differences between areas and establishing how registrations relate to the exhaust residents actually encounter. The supplied comments do not describe adjustment methods, individual exposure histories or uncertainty estimates.
- pengaru [[./thread#^c34657806|pengaru]] attributes a perceived Illinois–California exhaust difference to California fuel formulations and emissions controls. That explanation assumes these factors account for the experience; the account does not separate fuel, fleet composition, weather, route or traffic conditions.
- Claims about moving pollution away from streets assume that relocation and exhaust controls reduce human exposure overall. pengaru [[./thread#^c34657806|pengaru]] favors generation away from residences, and tomtheelder [[./thread#^c34657665|tomtheelder]] argues that pollution away from population centers is less harmful. Neither supplies exposure comparisons for people living or working near the alternative sources.
- Using tire replacement costs to infer particulate exposure requires several unsupported steps. hedora [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]] reports a high cost per mile, while dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658211|dangrossman]] reports different replacement experience; neither measures emitted particle mass, its fine-particle fraction or inhaled dose.
- Sensory discomfort and pollutant exposure need separate interpretation. blamazon [[./thread#^c34657500|blamazon]] describes unpleasant roadside dining, and pengaru [[./thread#^c34657806|pengaru]] describes noxious exhaust while cycling. These observations establish perceived conditions, but cannot identify PM2.5 or NO2 concentrations or quantify health risk.

## Conditions

- Geography may concentrate emissions: tablespoon [[./thread#^c34658508|tablespoon]] recalls mountains trapping California smog, and blamazon [[./thread#^c34658678|blamazon]] describes Los Angeles as a basin where ocean winds and surrounding mountains limit dispersion. blamazon also attributes pollution to extensive driving, stop-and-go traffic and short trips before catalytic converters warm up. These are explanatory claims rather than local air measurements.
- Vehicle enforcement differs by location. blamazon [[./thread#^c34657500|blamazon]] reports living in an unnamed US state without safety or emissions inspections. In a February 2023 account, Symbiote [[./thread#^c34659292|Symbiote]] reports London charges of £12.50 per day for older cars and £100 or £300 for older heavy trucks or buses. The thread does not measure the resulting exposure differences.
- Alternatives depend on urban form and safety. blamazon [[./thread#^c34658407|blamazon]] sees electrification as more achievable than quickly reversing US car dependence, while StreamBright [[./thread#^c34658355|StreamBright]] identifies existing European car-free dining areas. karmelapple [[./thread#^c34658216|karmelapple]] favors quiet e-bikes but notes that unsafe cycling environments discourage adoption.
- Travel time can constrain replacing car trips: nostromo123 [[./thread#^c34658168|nostromo123]] reports a commute outside a Central European capital taking 1.5 hours each way by train, metro and walking, versus 0.5 hours by car, despite frequent public transport. This is one person's practical constraint, without comparative pollution-exposure measurements.
- Affordability claims conflict. Responding to nickysielicki's assertion that EVs are all luxury cars [[./thread#^c34658013|nickysielicki]], fooblaster [[./thread#^c34666944|fooblaster]] reports buying a Chevrolet Bolt for \$28,000 after minimal rebates. That purchase challenges the blanket claim but does not establish affordability across households or places.

## Lived experiences

- pengaru [[./thread#^c34657806|pengaru]] reports noticeably more noxious exhaust while cycling during a summer family visit to Illinois than when riding in traffic after returning to California. This is a firsthand sensory comparison; no monitor readings, symptoms or medical outcomes accompany it.
- blamazon [[./thread#^c34657500|blamazon]] describes loud or damaged exhausts disrupting neighborhood outdoor dining. In the same discussion, ecf [[./thread#^c34669976|ecf]] recalls a Friday dinner in California's Central Valley interrupted roughly every five minutes by the same lifted diesel trucks revving repeatedly. These accounts document roadside discomfort and interrupted conversation, without measured particulate exposure.
- dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658159|dangrossman]] reports recurring difficulty hearing and being heard with a drive-through window open because nearby cars are idling. derefr [[./thread#^c34658601|derefr]] describes a noisy apartment beside an uphill four-lane road, including accelerating cars, truck brakes and motorcycles. These contexts challenge the claim that tire and wind noise explain virtually all urban vehicle disturbance.
- micromacrofoot [[./thread#^c34658291|micromacrofoot]] says they can feel a neighbor's stock approximately 2015 truck starting. Conversely, amalgamated\_inc [[./thread#^c34658467|amalgamated\_inc]] reports walking in cities where nearby cars mostly produce road noise and suggests reflective walls influence disturbance. The accounts concern different settings and vehicles, without a common measurement protocol.
- hedora [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]] reports EV tire wear costing \$0.15 per mile. In replies, blamazon [[./thread#^c34658347|blamazon]] reports paying \$1,100 for Model X tires and a previous identical set lasting about 48,000 miles; dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658211|dangrossman]] reports replacing OEM EV tires around 25,000 miles for about \$700. The new sets' projected lifetimes, including a 70,000-mile warranty, are conditional expectations rather than observed durability.

## Unresolved disagreements

- The relative importance of tailpipe, brake and tire pollution remains unquantified. melling's quoted tire-dominance claim [[./thread#^c34657764|melling]] and hedora's pollutant distinction [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]] contain no PM2.5 concentrations or size-resolved emissions. nickysielicki [[./thread#^c34658013|nickysielicki]] mentions NOx, without separate NO2 measurements. The supplied unit offers no air-sensor models, calibration checks or reliability comparisons.
- The magnitude and distribution of health benefits remain uncertain. dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658257|dangrossman]] reports a ZIP-code comparison of EV registrations and asthma-related emergency visits, while funstuff007 [[./thread#^c34658522|funstuff007]] questions whether trucks and other high emitters dominate particular locations. The comments provide no effect sizes or source-apportionment results to settle that concern.
- EV tire-cost reports do not establish a typical wear rate or exposure penalty. hedora's \$0.15-per-mile figure [[./thread#^c34657873|hedora]] differs substantially from the experiences discussed by blamazon [[./thread#^c34658347|blamazon]] and dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658211|dangrossman]]. The exchange does not determine whether the high figure reflects particular tires, driving conditions, a calculation issue or broader EV behavior.
- The acoustic benefit of electrification remains context-dependent in these accounts. amalgamated\_inc [[./thread#^c34659560|amalgamated\_inc]] emphasizes road noise and vehicle weight, while dangrossman [[./thread#^c34658159|dangrossman]] and derefr [[./thread#^c34658601|derefr]] identify idling and hills as substantial engine-noise situations. No common acoustic comparison resolves the disagreement, and sound levels cannot establish air-pollution reductions.
- Low population density does not resolve exposure for people still present. jjtheblunt's question about truck drivers and nearby motorists [[./thread#^c34658284|jjtheblunt]] remains unanswered after nickysielicki's rural-interstate claim [[./thread#^c34658013|nickysielicki]]. No firsthand measurements establish their doses or health risks.
- Indoor interventions and window-opening tradeoffs are unanswered in this unit. dangrossman's open-window account [[./thread#^c34658159|dangrossman]] concerns drive-through audibility, while hedora's CO2 link [[./thread#^c34657968|hedora]] concerns vehicle emissions accounting. Neither addresses indoor CO2, ventilation or filtration. No supplied account tests opening windows against outdoor pollution, cooking emissions or wildfire smoke.
- Comfort complaints do not establish cognition or sleep effects. blamazon [[./thread#^c34660858|blamazon]] mentions modified exhausts passing at 2 a.m., but reports no sleep outcome; ecf [[./thread#^c34669976|ecf]] describes interrupted dinner conversation. The supplied discussion contains no firsthand cognitive testing, sleep measurements or attribution of those outcomes to a particular air pollutant.
